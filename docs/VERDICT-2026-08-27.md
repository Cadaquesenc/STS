## The answer

**No go. Nothing found is within seven points of break-even — and a two-year
historical sweep says there was never anything to find.**

Revision 2 said "unresolved — 0.4 to 0.6 points short". **That was wrong, and it
rested on how a stop was priced.** The best result anyone found was a 5% trailing
stop on the most active coins. It was priced as though you get the stop level when
the stop fires. You do not: there is no resting stop order on pump.fun, so you
watch the price break, then send a transaction, and you fill at whatever the market
is doing by the time it lands.

Priced honestly, the same rule on the same 972 coins turns negative. **Four
reasonable ways of building "a 5% trailing stop" give −3.06% to −4.29% gross**,
which is **−4.96% to −6.19% net** against a +2.12% break-even. **The gap is about
7 to 8 points, not the 5.7 revision 3 claimed.** All four constructions are
positive when the fill is priced at the stop level (+2.20% to +9.73%) and negative
when it is priced where the stop actually fires, so the direction is not in doubt.

**Why this number moved three times, and the general lesson.** "A 5% trailing
stop" is not one rule. The running peak can track each second's closing price or
its high; the stop can be tested against the close or against the low. Those four
combinations spread the answer by six points — most of the whole 7-to-8-point gap to
break-even — and three separate passes each silently picked a different one. Any
future exit number in this project has to state both choices or it is not a number.

Do not start building execution. Do not spend money. Nothing here is a close call.

## It has never worked, in any market we can observe

This is the simplest statement of the result and it was the last thing found.

An hour-matched historical sweep — the third Wednesday of each month at 18:00 UTC,
October 2024 through August 2026, seven windows, each read from contiguous runs of
blocks with nothing missing — buys every launch and sells it inside the minute:

| | per trade |
|---|---|
| every one of the seven windows | **−6.5% to −17.1%** |
| mean | **−10.1%** |
| worst window | **May 2025** — the middle of the period, not the end |

**There is no decay curve. Expectancy was never positive.** The loss does not track
launch volume (r = −0.20), does not track how many rivals are in the first three
seconds (r = −0.11), and does not track time (r = −0.33). Because the windows are
hour-matched and drawn from unbroken block runs, the duty-cycle artefact that faked
every other era comparison in this project cannot be present here.

The sentence worth keeping: **in October 2024, with only 2.7 rival buyers in the
first three seconds, this trade lost 7%.** The edge was not competed away by faster
bots. There was never an edge to lose.

Two beliefs die with it. **The market did not collapse** — 43.9 launches a minute
in October 2024 against 37.6 a minute in August 2026, roughly 1.6 million a month
at both ends. And **the evidence was never out of reach**: the free Helius endpoint
serves full blocks, with transactions and logs, back to at least August 2024, at
zero cost. Nobody had swept it.

**Limits, stated plainly:** hour 18 UTC only, 110–183 launches per window (so ±2.7
to ±10.2 points), 7 of 23 planned windows, landed transactions only, and before
own-order impact and priority fees.

## The number

Buy every launch, enter at second 3, sell by second 60, pay the pump.fun round
trip. Seven independent methods, seven different fields, run without sight of each
other:

| Who | Method | Per trade |
|---|---|---|
| W8 | full population, n=9,418 | **−7.8%**  [−9.9, −5.6] |
| W9 | second-by-second price paths, n=6,989 | **−8.9%**  [−10.4, −7.4] |
| W11 | session-corrected, truncation filtered, n=7,935 | **−9.9%** |
| W2 | `outcome` summary fields, per day | −10.5% / −10.5% / −3.0% |
| W5 | buying everything on a social gate | −9.0% |
| W10 | hold to end of window | −12.6% |
| W28 | curve-verified coins only, n=3,743 | **−11.9%** |

A red team reproduced W8's figure exactly (−7.76%, same n). The agreement is worth
more than any single number.

The best exit rule out of 108 tested on real price paths loses **2.32%**, and that
figure carries the fill artefact this document retires elsewhere — it prices a
trailing stop as though it fills *at* the stop level. The same grid on scrambled
seconds gives −2.75% to −3.21%, which made all the real structure in the data look
worth about **0.4 to 0.9 percentage points**.

**Do not quote that gap as a measured quantity.** Re-run on a 207-rule grid the sign
reverses: real scores −0.51% and the scrambled corpora **+0.61% to +1.34%**, so the
scramble beats the real data by 1.86 points. Different grids, and the scramble count
was a free choice in both. The conclusion is the same either way and it is the only
part to carry: **there is no exploitable time structure here.** Quote the direction,
not the size.

**Break-even is +2.12% gross at 0.05 SOL** — the verified 1.90% pump.fun round
trip plus 0.22% of network fee. (Earlier passes used 2.22%, from a 2.00% fee. The
real fee is 95 basis points a side, measured on 4,918 sells, so 2.12% is the
figure — and it makes the bar slightly *easier*, and it still is not cleared.)
**The best thing found, priced with realistic fills, is −3.06% to −4.29% gross and
−4.96% to −6.19% net.** The gap is 7 to 8 points — more than twice the ±3.1
resolution of this dataset.

Two tables state the whole problem without argument. First, entry timing
(n=5,410, exit at window end, gross):

| entry | mean | net | win rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0s | −8.74% | −10.64% | 11.1% |
| 3s | −8.22% | −10.12% | 9.6% |
| 10s | −5.25% | −7.15% | 7.9% |
| 30s | −0.86% | −2.76% | 6.2% |
| 50s | +0.31% | −1.59% | 4.2% |

The mean climbs toward zero only because entering later means holding for less
time — the win rate falls the whole way. **Net is negative at every entry second.**
The population bleeds at a roughly constant rate and the round trip is a fixed toll
you cannot outrun by shortening your stay.

Second, the same signal seen from both ends. This is the **full** table — an
earlier revision printed it without the 4–7 row, which is the row that keeps it
honest (n=5,386, recomputed for this revision):

| early buyers | n | mean PEAK | mean END |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1,903 | 1.069 | **0.985** |
| 2–3 | 1,200 | 1.237 | 0.911 |
| 4–7 | 1,084 | 1.206 | 0.884 |
| 8–15 | 734 | 1.274 | 0.840 |
| 16+ | 465 | **1.336** | 0.905 |

**On the real holdout, half of this table survives and half does not — so state
the halves separately.** **The finish column is the durable half**: on 08-16 every
bucket still finishes below 1.0 and loses 5 to 21 points, exactly as here. **The
peak ladder is in-sample only** — 08-16 peaks at the **2–3** bucket (1.749) rather
than climbing to the top. This sentence has now been corrected three times; **the
version to carry forward is "more early interest means a worse finish", and not
anything about the shape of the peak column.**

**The peak rises monotonically. The finish falls, then goes flat at the top.**
That is the claim the *in-sample* table supports and it is all it supports. It is *not*
"monotone in both directions at once", and this sentence has now been corrected
twice: once for being printed without the 4–7 row that breaks the peak column, and
again for the end column, which **breaks clearly at 4–7 once the hygiene rule is
lifted** and where the top bucket rises to **0.905** — a −11.19% net trade. (The
end column was already flat at the top *before* the correction, 0.839 against
0.840; the correction sharpened the break lower down rather than creating one.)
Every bucket still finishes below 1.0. The finding underneath survives both: **more early interest buys a
higher spike and a worse close, and the coins that finish best are the ones where
nothing happens.** Those still lose, because 0.985 minus 1.90% is a loss. **Quiet
coins cost you the fee; active coins cost you more. No bucket wins.**

(`open.sellers` does *not* reproduce this shape — its finish column has no trend
at all and its peak collapses in the top bucket. An earlier note claiming it
reproduced the table exactly was wrong, and the field has since been withdrawn on
separate evidence.)

## Who actually makes money here: the creator, and essentially nobody else

Every other section asks whether *our* strategy would profit. This one asks whether
**anyone** does, and it is the most useful thing the investigation produced.

On 5,241 curve-verified launches (82,793 wallet rows, 21,971 wallets):

| | staked | ended | return |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Creators** | 13,130 SOL | +3,780 | **+28.8%** |
| **Everyone else** | 62,235 SOL | −5,063 | **−8.1%** |

On the 66.6% of launches where the creator dumps everything inside 60 seconds they
make **+39.2% on stake**, mean +1.02 SOL, median +0.18, win rate 60% — replicating
on every day with token data (+46%, +40%, +30%), held-out day included.
Non-creators who also fully exit make **−0.4% ± 0.9**, which is zero.

**That +39% is the number to quote, because it needs no assumption about open
positions.** The +28.8% above does — it marks unsold bags pro-rata. An independent
rebuild confirmed every figure here to the decimal (+29.5% / −7.2%) and then tested
the obvious objection: does valuing unsold bags at zero punish non-creators, who
hold, while flattering creators, who dumped? **It is the other way round.** Creators
still hold 19.3% of what they bought, and their bags are the cheapest tokens in the
coin because they bought in the launch transaction itself — a creator's bag at last
price is 97.7% of their stake against 47.0% for everyone else. The more generously
open positions are marked, the *wider* the creator's edge runs: 24.5 points at zero,
36.7 pro-rata, 75.2 at last price. What the objection does establish is that the
creator *level* moves with the assumption even though the ranking does not — all
creators taken together, with bags at zero, are −2.94%.

**And the edge is the seat, not the operator: when creator wallets buy somebody
else's coin, they lose 4.03%.**

**And winners persist — but only creators.** Return-on-stake correlation between the
halves of the corpus is +0.61 to +0.65 against a shuffled null of +0.01 ± 0.03
(z ≈ +23). That looks like a real edge until it is split: creator rows give +0.86 to
+0.94, and their first-half winners earn **+38% to +42%** in the second half.
Non-creator rows give the *same* +0.61 correlation and a second-half mean of
**−0.1% ± 1.6**. Past non-creator winners keep winning more often — 50% against a
23% base rate — **at exactly zero expectancy.**

That is the sentence worth keeping: **a persistent style, not a persistent profit.**
Skill is visible in this market and it is not worth anything.

**It is negative-sum.** Only 1.39% of coins come near graduation, so almost no
outside money enters — that is what carries the claim. (An adversarial recheck
trimmed the supporting arithmetic: `in − out = locked` is an identity rather than a
finding, and the ratio-1.000 agreement between price-implied and flow-implied curve
balances is partly circular, since the curve check selects for it. It does still
establish that the fee sits outside the staked total.)

But the platform is not the main drain, and the natural assumption here is wrong:
pump.fun's fees are **11–13% of total winnings**, not more than them.
**Per 100 SOL a non-creator stakes, 1.7 goes to pump.fun and 6.5 goes to the
creator.** The creator extracts about four times what the platform does. That is the
downward drift measured everywhere else in this document, seen from the other side —
it is not friction, it is somebody's income.

Three findings that settle the strategy question from this angle:

1. The only positive entry bucket is **the launch block itself** (+9.5%), declining
   monotonically after.
2. **At our size it pays nothing even there.** The median same-block buyer at
   0.05–0.5 SOL returns **exactly −1.90%** — the round trip and nothing else. (Two
   fees of 95 bps are 1.90% added and 1.89% compounded; both figures appear in these
   documents and they are the same toll.) That
   is not a coincidence but an identity: 2,175 of 2,672 such rows make exactly two
   trades, and buying then selling on a constant-product curve returns your SOL
   exactly, so the loss *is* the toll. The *median* only turns positive at 5+ SOL
   (+18.1%); the *mean* is already positive at 0.5–1 SOL (+7.0%) and 2–5 SOL
   (+11.0%) and reaches +34% at 5+, because it is a fat right tail with a losing
   middle. An earlier revision put the median and the mean in one sentence and read
   "positive only at 5+" off both. Either way it is a *size* effect this bankroll
   cannot reach: a 5 SOL order is five times the whole of it.

   **Two reports disagree on the mean here, and agree on the median.** Measured a
   different way — only wallets that fully closed a position, SOL-weighted, joined
   through the fee data — the same size band gives a mean of **+6.4% gross** on
   n=704 rather than −1.7% on n=2,683. Both put the *median* trade at or below the
   round trip, so both say the typical trade loses; the disagreement lives entirely
   in the right tail, and the smaller sample flags itself as optimistic because
   dropping wallets that never got out drops exactly the losers. Nobody reconciled
   them. A €200 bankroll cannot ride a distribution whose middle trade is negative
   under either reading.
3. **The professionals lose too.** 1,396 non-creator wallets appearing in 10+
   launches staked 27,558 SOL and lost 5.6%.

Honesty note carried from that work: marking open positions at last price values the
outstanding bags at 41,653 SOL against 16,159 SOL of curve money to pay them — 2.58x
impossible. Results are therefore reported at bags-worth-zero and pro-rata, never at
last price. `who[]` also stops at about 60 seconds, so the 32% of rows still holding
are the real uncertainty, and across all seven days the bags-worth-zero bound on
creators is −15.1% — so the +39% belongs specifically to the launch-and-dump subset,
not to creators as a class.

## Wall 1 — we arrive after the move, and we cannot fix that with money

The median coin's best moment is exactly break-even. Half of all coins that trade
at all never rise a single tick.

- **Peaks land early: 60.7% of coins hit their 60-second high at or before second
  3, 69.8% by second 5, 91.3% by second 30** (full priced population, n=6,966).
  An earlier revision said "76% within 5 seconds". That is 08-21 alone — the
  quietest day in the corpus, where 46% of priced coins never move a tick. By day
  the 5-second figure runs 60.9% / 70.8% / 76.5%.
- **66–79% of coins have already hit their 60-second high at or before the second
  we are able to decide.**
- 21% never trade at all; 38.5% are dead by second 3.

Three separate attempts to blame this on the 60-second capture window all failed.
Tick data out to 300 seconds gives an identical 1.5x crossing rate (31.6% → 31.6%)
and an identical 2x rate (17.9% → 17.9%). Holding longer is monotonically worse:
−11.6% / −14.8% / −16.1% at 60 / 120 / 300 seconds. Only 5.1% of coins peak after
second 60. And the 12-hour label — which existed on disk the whole time — says
holding past a minute loses a further 5.2 points pooled — 2.5 points on 08-16,
5.2 on 08-20, 5.4 on 08-21, so it is every session but not the same size in each —
even though those later hours contain half of all 3x events. Graded with a real
exit rule rather than a naive hold, the extra eleven hours cost less and still
cost: 0.32 points, for five hundred times the capital lockup.

**Speed is not purchasable here, and would not help anyway.** Two findings, from
opposite directions:

- Entering at second 3 and second 5 make the *same money*. The opportunity decays
  1.4 points a second but what any rule can capture decays 0.1–0.3.
- **Paying more does not buy an earlier slot.** Paired within the same launch, the
  higher-fee transaction landed earlier **50.3%** of the time inside the first 25
  slots — the only slots anyone fights over — on **76,795** such pairs. A coin
  flip. Over all 303,681 pairs at any distance it is 52.3%: statistically real,
  economically nothing. The chance of landing in the launch slot
  by fee paid is a U-shape, not a ramp: zero fee 18.3%, 10–50k lamports 0.5%,
  1–5M 19.0%. Splitting each wallet's *own* trades into cheap and expensive halves
  moves the odds only 2.1% → 4.5%, so the top of that U is *which wallet it is*,
  not what it paid. The front of this market is won by Jito bundles, not by bidding.

And we appear not to be in that race at all. **94.7% of late buyers — 1,169 of
1,234 wallets — never once land in a launch slot**; only 65 do both. That figure
reproduces exactly, holds on `feePayer` (94.8%), corroborates independently at
n=23,816, and is not an artefact of counting: two null models, one of which
shuffles arrival positions within each coin, both say the overlap should have been
about 210 wallets rather than 65. **The split is a real property of wallets.**

**What it does not prove is the thing it was used to prove.** "Never landed early"
is not "never tried". Nothing in landed-only data distinguishes a sprayer from a
single-shot buyer — multi-landing bursts are indistinguishable across the two
populations (6.40% against 6.28%) — and the fee test discriminates *backwards*:
pure-late wallets bid **harder** when they land late (median 105,000 lamports) than
known racers do (53,000), and 12% of them pay the 1,005,000 sniper preset at least
once. Only the ~22% who never bid above 15,000 lamports are provably not racing.
So: on a free public RPC from a home laptop we are in the late group, and the
honest reason is latency, not a demonstrated absence of intent.

## Two things this verdict does NOT say

**First: the winners in this dataset are largely not real winners — and not, as
first thought, because the decoder was wrong.**

Token reserves reconcile trade-to-trade 99.76% of the time. **SOL reserves only
84.1%.** On 18.4% of testable coins the recorded price moves further than the money
that traded could possibly have moved it, or drops below the launch floor — a
corrupt `virtualSolReserves`. The defect is concentrated almost entirely in the
tail: on this *curve-consistency* check, **9 of the 10, 23 of the 25 and 79 of the
100 largest peaks fail.** P(5x) is 0.15% among coins that pass and 3.51% among
coins that fail. Among failing coins peaking ≥2x the peak would need a median 21.9
SOL and only 3.7 SOL ever arrived. (A second, simpler *money* test below reaches
the same tail from a different direction and agrees; the two ladders are two
checks, not one number quoted twice.)

Removing them moves every number **the wrong way for the strategy**: pooled
−9.84% → **−11.91%** (on n=3,743 — an earlier table said 4,790; the figure itself
reproduces exactly), P(2x) 6.8% → 3.2%, largest peak 34.1x → 11.7x. On the
held-out day, −5.49% → −7.20%. (The "best coin in the day is 2.7x" that used to
sit here was an artefact of a hygiene rule, not of either filter — the best genuine
coin peaks at **3.12x**. See below.)

That is the curve-consistency filter, which can only judge coins that have candles.
**A second filter, built on the actor's mechanism rather than on price
arithmetic, can judge all of them — and its answer is the most useful single result
in this section.** It is below, under *What the corpus looks like cleaned*.

**And it is not a recording error — those prices really printed.** Take the reserve
pair the *previous* event reported, apply the constant product to the next trade's
SOL, and predict its token amount. That matches **99.4%** of the affected trades
while predicting from the launch curve matches **2.9%**; a fee-paying control gives
96.9% and 90.1%. The chain itself priced each following trade off the "impossible"
number, so it was the live pool state. This was rebuilt clean-room — own borsh
reader, own base58, none of the capture code — and holds on four further windows
(99.7/2.9, 100/2.7, 99.2/1.6).

**One piece of evidence for that had to be withdrawn, and the replacement is
better.** An earlier revision said an independent reader agreed with the decoder on
every field of every stored raw event. True, and beside the point: all 30 surviving
raws have `sol == 0` and `feeBasisPoints == 95`, and **not one of the 1,704 zero-fee
trades kept a raw at all** — the samples say nothing about the affected population.
What actually clears the decoder needs no raws: the `creator` field at byte 169
matches the coin's launch creator on **3,005 of 3,007** zero-fee trades, which it
could not do if the offsets were wrong. (`realSolReserves` at byte 105 also decodes
cleanly on **11,142 of 11,142** live trades — which is why the recorder now writes
it.)

**The marker is exact: every affected trade has `feeBasisPoints == 0` and
`fee == 0`.** 92–95% of normal 95-bps trades obey the launch curve. It is sticky —
14.1% of trades are zero-fee but 21.1% end up off-curve, touching 17.2% of
multi-trade coins.

**But zero-fee and manufactured-price are two events, not one, and an earlier
revision ran them together.** In the 2025-11-19 onset window all **142** of the
actor's zero-fee trades have `|Δ virtualSolReserves| ÷ sol` equal to **exactly
1.000** — every one of them on-curve. **The fee exemption came first; price
manufacturing began later, from February 2026 onward.** So `fee == 0` is a marker
and not a law, the two checks are not synonyms, and anyone filtering on one should
apply the other beside it rather than assume it follows.

**The mechanism, precisely.** The token side is correct. What is wrong is that the
**quoted SOL reserve is rescaled in proportion to the curve's real SOL balance
rather than moved by the trade amount.** An ordinary trade moves the reserve
1.000x its own SOL; these move it a **median 55x, p90 303x**. The worked case is a
coin quoting a 95-SOL curve while holding 2.51 SOL — and when the creator exits, he
takes out 2.514584 SOL against a computed balance of 2.514582. **Two lamports.**
The SOL amounts are real and the money is real; only the quote is fiction. Three of
the 52 coins it touched crossed pump.fun's 115-SOL graduation threshold while
holding under 0.6 SOL. **And it is the ordinary `buy` and `sell` instruction** —
no promotional path, no variant the recorder cannot see — established by decoding
the instruction-name string in the trailing bytes of the 2026 event and confirmed
on 20,157 trades with perfect agreement.

**It is one operator and one program, and the onset is datable.** An earlier
revision of this section said "a single wallet". It is not a wallet:
`BwWK17cbHxwWBKZkUYvzxLcNQ1YVyaFezduWbtm2de6s` has 32 bytes that are off the
ed25519 curve, so it is **a program address with no private key**. Behind it is one
ordinary wallet, sole signer and fee payer of all 1,704 zero-fee transactions,
which never once appears as the trading `user`.

Dating it: in **August 2025 the fee fields were present and reading 95 bps, with 0
zero-fee trades out of 19,097** — so the absence then is real, not a layout
artefact, and the onset falls between 2025-08-20 and 2025-11-19. Since then: 145
trades in November 2025, 520 in February 2026, 792 in August 2026 — **1.3% → 3.9%
→ 5.4% of all market trades.** (One narrower caveat does stand: the October 2024
count of zero cannot be read as evidence either way, because the fee field did not
exist in that event layout — the tail grew from 16 to 150 bytes across eleven
variants over the two years.)

Cleaning the August 2026 window moves it from −10.9% ±15.1 to **−14.7% ±10.2**.
The correction makes the loss *worse*, which is the direction every correction in
this document has run.

**Be fair about what it is, because it is easy to write this unfairly.** Over seven
days it moved about 3,700 SOL in and 3,778 out — **+1.7%, which is market-maker
economics, not a robbery.** Buyers on its coins do slightly worse at the median
(0.69 returned against 0.75) and **clearly better in the tail**: about a third more
get their money back and about a third fewer are wiped out completely, because it
is a two-sided counterparty that will buy your bag when the coin is one second old.
**It sells a chart. It does not appear to rob the audience.**

**Why it matters here** is narrower and sharper: it appears in five capture windows
over ten months and in STS's own `who[]` on **all seven days, held-out 08-21
included**; it arrives as the second wallet about a second after launch; it does
67–75% of a touched coin's trades and 37–49% of its SOL; and it is present in **10
of the 10 biggest peaks in the corpus.** On the held-out day it touches 17.9% of
tracked coins and supplies **86% of everything that doubled and 94% of everything
above 3x** — 15 of 16. **Nearly every winner in our held-out data is a manufactured
chart.**

**Detecting it costs nothing and is now done at capture time**: `fee == 0 && sol >
0`, and `|Δ virtualSolReserves| ÷ sol` must equal 1.000. Two checks.

So the fat tail this project was built to ride is not a decoding artefact and it is
not a fortune we mislaid. **It is real prints on coins nobody's money could pay for
and nobody could have exited into** — a 34x visible on screen with no liquidity
behind it. The test that confirms it from the money side is the simplest one in
this document — **could the money that entered this coin have paid for the price it
printed?** — and it is the most concrete version of the whole finding: **the
corpus's single biggest winner, a 34.1x, took in 17.7766 SOL and paid out 17.7766
SOL, exactly zero net money, while its recorded price claimed 149.3 SOL of
inflow.** Applied to every coin it is monotone in the peak:

| peak | fails the money test |
|---|---|
| all coins | 15.1% |
| top 100 peaks | 66 of 100 |
| top 50 | 39 of 50 |
| top 25 | 22 of 25 |
| **top 10** | **10 of 10** |
| above 10x | **88.2%** |

**Two traps if anyone re-runs it.** The ceiling has to be **gross** `solIn`, not
net — net fires on 73% of everything and means nothing — and STS's recorded price
scale changes units on 08-15, so a single threshold across the corpus is wrong.

(An earlier revision cited a *token*-balance version instead: "100% of coins above
10x record a peak at which more tokens left the curve than were bought". **Do not
use it.** The base rate reproduces at 7.3%, but the band table does not — 75% above
10x rather than 100%, 45.7% at 5–10x rather than 88.9%, resting on six coins. A
separate re-implementation over `who[]` could not reproduce it at all and found a
smooth tail with no separation, consistent with a buy-leg / sell-leg accounting
difference rather than a defect. The money test above is the one that holds.)

### What the corpus looks like cleaned

Remove the actor's coins — by mechanism, not by price arithmetic, so the days
without candles can be judged too — and 17.3% of the corpus goes (7,971 → 6,595).

**The average moves by less than a point in a direction this data does not
determine. The tail collapses.**

| | full corpus | cleaned |
|---|---|---|
| **mean per trade** | **−9.84%** | **−7.76%** |
| P(1.5x) | 13.59% | 8.17% * |
| P(2x) | 6.80% | 3.47% * |
| P(3x) | 2.67% | **1.37%** |
| P(5x) | 0.92% | **0.420%** |
| P(10x) | 0.18% | 0.02% * |
| biggest peak | 34.1x | **11.66x** |

\* These three rows still carry a hygiene rule this sprint should never have
applied (below), so they are each slightly too low. The three bold rows are what
you get without it. **Nothing changes sign either way**: the corpus is negative at
every entry second under both, every activity selector is still worse than buying
everything, and the held-out day is still negative.

**The sentence to take from that table is that the actor carried the *variance*,
not the *returns*.** Actor rows are 17.3% of the sample and **82.2% of the total
sum of squares** — 22 times the variance per coin. The mechanism is visible: the
actor is **73.8% of the worst-finishing decile and 41.6% of the best**, and only
1–8% of the middle. It sits on both ends, so deleting it is close to symmetric
about the mean.

**Do not over-read the size of the move on the mean.** Under the sprint's own
hygiene rule the two columns differed by one hundredth of a point, and that was a
pooling coincidence — the per-day shifts run from **−1.49 points (08-21) to +1.41
points (08-16)** and happened to cancel, the actor arm's own standard error is 4.5
points, and **on the held-out day removing the actor makes the result worse**
(−5.49% → −6.98%). Corrected, the move is about two points in the other direction.
Neither reading is worth leaning on. The defensible claim is the one about
variance, above.

What is not in doubt is the tail. The actor supplies **58% of all doublers, 75% of
the 3x, 81% of the 5x and 93% of the 10x**, and on the held-out day **86% of the
doublers and 94% of everything above 3x** (15 of 16).

**This is the sentence the whole section exists for:** *the average trade was
losing for reasons that have nothing to do with the actor — what dies when you
remove it is the fat tail the entire case rested on.*

**The correction under that table is the sprint's own fault and it is worth being
precise about.** Every pass dropped rows where `len(outcome.highs) >= 60`
(see *What the data can and cannot settle*). It removes **half of all genuine 5x
coins**. Without it the pooled cleaned expectancy is **−7.76% rather than −9.85%**
— a **2.087-point** move — and **a coin reaches 5x one time in 238, not one in
471.** That figure was quoted prominently in this document and was out by a factor
of two. **Quote the 5x rate with its range, not as a point:** it moves from
0.212% to 0.420%, but that is 14 coins to 28, and the Wilson intervals **overlap**.
P(3x) — 0.819% to 1.366% — has disjoint intervals and is the safer of the two to
lean on. The collapse of the tail is real and survives; its most vivid number was
about twice as steep as the truth.

**And the best genuine coin on the held-out day peaks at 3.12x, not 2.71x.** It has
82 wallets, 156 trades and 36.4 SOL of inflow against the 22.96 its peak required,
so it is real by every test in this document — and it was removed by that same
hygiene rule rather than by anything to do with the actor.

**Two filters built on completely different principles agree to three decimals.**
On the held-out day the mechanism filter gives **−6.98%** and the curve-consistency
filter **−6.99%**, and under identical hygiene both name the same coin as the day's
best. They overlap heavily but are not the same set — P(curve-bad | actor) is
99.5%, Jaccard 44.8% — so they are reported separately and combined, never stacked.
The mechanism filter's unique contribution is the **2,098 coins from 08-10 to 08-15
that the curve filter cannot test at all**, including the corpus's 34.1x. That the
two converge on a population one of them cannot even see is the strongest evidence
either is right.

**And the strongest confirmation in this section uses no address at all.** A third
detector — the gross SOL-flow bound, which only asks whether the money that entered
could have paid for the price, and never looks at who traded — flags **846 coins**
and gives **P(2x) 3.44%, P(5x) 0.211% and the same 11.66x maximum.** It agrees with
the address-based filter to three decimals **while removing 40% fewer coins**, and
P(actor | flow-bad) is **98.6%**. Three routes, one of them address-blind, one
population-blind, all landing on the same corpus.

**And there is a detector so clean it is worth a line on its own: all 294 coins
whose entry price sits below their own launch price are the actor's. One hundred
percent.** A coin cannot open below its own floor. That single comparison, free at
capture time, finds a fifth of the affected population with no false positives in
this corpus.

**Both structural claims in this document get stronger, not weaker.** Cleaned, the
volatility-not-direction table sharpens — the peak now climbs 1.020 → 1.337 across
the buyer buckets rather than 1.069 → 1.337, with the finishes flat — and every
entry-second cell moves further negative (second 3 goes −10.12% → **−10.46%**),
with win rates falling in every bucket. The matched-exposure version is negative
throughout as well.

**Limits, stated plainly.** No STS coin carries per-trade fee or reserve data, and
flux and STS share zero mints, so **the detector's error rate on this corpus is
unmeasured** — there are three corroborations and none of them is an exact join.
99 coins hide behind the 200-wallet cap. Everything here remains before own-order
impact.

### The tail is smaller than it looked. It is not empty.

The paragraphs above are the strongest negative in this document and they should
not be read as more than they are, because the same verification turned up
something nobody was looking for:

> **86 coins peaked above 3x with 50 or more distinct wallets in them. Not one of
> those 86 fails the money test, and not one contains that actor's program.**

That reproduces to the decimal, every cell of both tables. One caveat the first
pass omitted: 26 of the 86 have no recorded `curve{}`, so their money test assumes
the standard 30-SOL curve; the other 60 pass against their own. By day: 53 on
08-20, 18 on 08-11, 14 on 08-16, and **1 on the held-out day.**

**There is a real tail.** It is **86 coins — 0.70% of 12,205 launches** — and what
separates it from the manufactured one is not a curve-consistency test at all. It
is **how many different wallets were there**, which is free and available at
capture time. The dataset is not uniformly poisoned. A reader should not leave this
section believing every large move in this market is fake, because they are not.

**And a genuine 3x looks nothing like the thing this project was built to catch.**
It is a **40-second climb, not a 3-second spike**: median 175 wallets, 394 trades,
154 SOL in, peaking at **second 39** and still at 3.21x when the window closes.
**Not one of the 86 peaked at or before second 3.** Over twelve hours their median
is −25.8%, so it is a 60-second object and not an investment.

**It is not decidable at second 3, and the measurement points the wrong way.**
Inside any second-3 bucket, the coins that went on to run had **fewer** early
buyers (AUC 0.349), **less** early SOL (0.385) and **fewer** early trades (0.366).
Seven features tested, **every AUC below 0.5** — the early signals are not weakly
predictive of the real tail, they are *anti*-predictive of it.

**This also settles a contradiction this document has been carrying**: that many
early wallets marks the real tail, while `open.wallets >= 15` returns −2.3%. They
are two halves of one table. As `open.wallets` climbs from 1 to 30, the chance a
coin *ends* with 50+ wallets rises **10.8% → 76.1%**, while the chance it peaks
above 3x stays flat near 3.5% up to twelve and then **collapses to 1.2%**. Early
hands buy you the "many wallets" half of the definition and cost you the "3x"
half. The best precision anywhere on that grid is **5.1% against a 9.2%
break-even**, and every tightening raises the bar faster than it raises precision.

**Graded as a strategy it fails exactly like everything else.** Best of 433
realistic-fill cells: **+1.99% gross, +0.09% net** — and that sits at the **29th
percentile of its own shuffled null**, whose median is +3.89%. Drop the single best
coin, which is 53% of all the profit, and it is **−0.96% net**; the median trade is
**−5.83%**. On the held-out day every filter and every exit is negative, and every
filter is *worse* than buying everything (−3.55% unfiltered against −7.68% at
`open.wallets >= 10`).

**Nothing is recoverable, and the reason is the sharpest lesson of the night.**
The recorder stored only derived open/high/low/close prices. Zero coins can be
repaired. But the missing numbers were never missing from the wire, and never
missing from the parser either: **the decoder had been reading `realSolReserves`,
`realTokenReserves`, `feeBasisPoints` and `fee` off every trade since the day it
was written — `watch.js` simply never wrote them out.** A quarter of this dataset's
tail is permanently unusable for want of four values that were sitting in a
variable at the moment the row was serialised. The recorder now writes the full
curve state on every candle (`vsol`, `vtok`, `rsol`, `rtok`) plus a zero-fee
census, at a cost of **+385 bytes on a mean 4.35 KB record** — which is the entire
price of the fix, and it was always the entire price. Record raw state, not derived
state.

**And this is a limit on the numbers in this document, not only on future work.**
Every multiple above about 1.5x in every section above — the hit-rate ladders, the
social buckets, the 2x and 3x rates, the tail counts, the single best trades that
carried the filters — is measured against prices that are wrong on roughly one coin
in five and cannot be repaired. Nobody rebuilt them because nobody can. What saves
the conclusion is that the error runs one way: **every time the affected coins are
removed, every headline gets worse.** So the no-go is safe. Any number in this
document that depends on a coin having *risen a long way* is not.

(A related scare is closed: the
`virtualSol: 4.292` cheap-launch variant is genuine, not corrupt — but all 216 such
coins carry no outcome at all, so they inflate nothing. Do not filter on it.)

**Second: the stated thesis was tested too, and it is worse.**

The README describes STS as an engine for *"the difficult middle of the market:
approximately $25k–$80k market-cap bonding curves and Raydium consolidation"* — the
venue in that sentence is also wrong, graduates migrate to PumpSwap — and
says it *"does not chase zero-MEV launch races"*. Everything above is the launch
window, so the obvious defence is that the sprint answered the wrong question.

**It does not survive.** Market cap is computable from the curve (supply 1e9,
launch ≈ 27.96 SOL, graduation ≈ 410.8 SOL), and anchoring on pump.fun's own $69k
graduation figure puts **$25k at 5.32x launch — with $80k *above* graduation.** So
the stated band is really "5.3x up to graduation", and it is testable, thinly.

**Start with the part that needs no statistics at all, because it settles the
question on its own.**

A pump.fun graduation deposits **206,900,000 tokens and 67.405853768 SOL** into a
new PumpSwap pool — **50 of the 54 readable pool-creation transactions deposit
exactly those two numbers**, and all 65 graduates in the corpus have a pool. The
bonding curve's last price is 115 virtual SOL against 279.9M virtual tokens.
Divide the two out:

| | implied market cap |
|---|---|
| last price on the bonding curve | 410.9 SOL (~$69k) |
| price the new pool opens at | 325.8 SOL (~$55k) |
| **difference** | **−20.71%** |

**The token side needed no network at all.** 206,900,000 is
`supply − curve.realTokens`, declared in every coin's own captured record. Half of
this constant was sitting in the data the whole sprint.

**Almost every coin loses a fifth of its value at the instant it graduates** —
pump.fun keeps about 17.6 of the 85 SOL. "Every single one, deterministically" is
one word too strong, and the exceptions are worth the line: **2 of 54 (4%)** deposit
82.0 and 85.1 SOL instead, one of them through a legacy `Migrate` instruction,
giving **−3.5% and +0.1%**. So the honest figures are **median −20.71%, mean
−18.34%** — still roughly double the **9.60%** giveback that decides this section,
so nothing downstream moves.

The statistical version agrees and then some: **the median 12-hour giveback is
−99.64%** (n=65) — an independent recomputation gives −99.52% — and the median
graduate is worth **0.36% of its graduation price** twelve hours later. **Those
marks are read from pool reserves, not from last-trade prices**, so a stale or dead
pool is still valued correctly: 7 of the 65 pools have had no trade since the
12-hour mark, and their marks are exactly today's state.

**So buying the band is worse than buying everything.** Not equal to it, and not
"thin but losing" — worse. The −6.53% gross / −8.43% net figure (n=86) is what you
get if you assume you sell at the graduation cap, and **that price ceases to exist
at the moment of migration.**

**Re-run with an exit a trader could actually take** — hold through migration and
sell into the fresh pool — the band returns **−17.94% gross, −19.84% net** against
**−12.20% / −14.10%** for buying everything. That is where a 20.71% giveback
predicts it should land, so the measurement and the arithmetic agree. **But do not
quote the 5.7-point gap between those means: it is not significant** (CI [−24.1,
+13.6]), and a mean on this population is one coin wearing a disguise.

**The statistic that does settle it is compounding, which is also what a trader
actually experiences.** Staking the same amount trade after trade:

| | compounded |
|---|---|
| buy everything | **−29.52%** |
| **buy at the band** | **−58.62%** gross, **−59.41%** net |
| gap | **−29.1 points**, CI **[−39.1, −16.5]** — excludes zero |

Median band trade **−77.9%** against **−5.1%**. And the mean is exactly the trap it
looks like: the band's +74.75% under measured prices is **one coin** — drop it and
the band is **−59.74%**.

**Depth finishes it.** The median band graduate's pool holds **1.80 SOL** at twelve
hours, and **1 of 17** band graduates is above its graduation price. You could not
have sold into these marks even at the marks.

Three things settle it:

- **Only 2.48% of launches ever graduate** — 99 of a random 4,000, CI 1.99–2.96%.
  That is the base rate any "middle of the market" strategy is working against, and
  it does not appear anywhere else in this project.
- **Winners do not keep.** Of 65 graduates, 5 were above their graduation price at
  twelve hours, 3 at twenty-four, and **one today**. One reached a $29M market cap
  at twelve hours; its pool now holds 0.4 SOL.
- **You could not have sold in any case.** The median graduate's pool holds **1.37
  SOL** at twelve hours, so even the losing marks above are optimistic.

**The hole in this, stated plainly:** about **45% of graduations happen after the
12-hour observation window** (16 flagged against 29 pools in a 1,297-coin
cross-check), so **the measured giveback figures describe the fast half only.** It
does not threaten the conclusion — the −20.71% at migration is deterministic and
applies to every graduate whenever it happens — but the twelve-hour numbers are a
subset, not the population.

**A correction that matters, because the first version of this section was wrong.**
An earlier pass reported −62% and "48 points worse than buying everything", with a
monotone gradient across thresholds. Both were artefacts of how *graduated* coins
were handled, and an adversarial recheck reproduced the original arithmetic exactly
before finding it:

| assumption about graduates | buy everything | buy at $25k |
|---|---|---|
| sold at graduation cap | −11.40% | **−6.53%** |
| deleted | −13.89% | −51.53% |
| assumed worthless | −15.28% | −61.11% |

Graduates are **17 of 86 in the band arm (19.8%) against 65 of 4,046 in the
baseline (1.61%)** — a **12.3x** asymmetry, so deleting them removes a fifth of one
arm's winners and one in sixty of the other's. **That asymmetry is now measured
rather than reasoned**: it never falls below **11x** across four differently
constructed populations. (An earlier pass argued it from first principles — a coin
only graduates by rising a long way, so graduates live inside the band by
construction — because the check meant to measure it had failed. The argument was
right; it is better to have the number.) The
same deletion also manufactured the monotone gradient — the "trend" tracks the
graduate share of each bucket (4.3% → 100% as the floor rises), and under the
sell-at-graduation assumption the curve is flat at about −5%. And they are real
coins: 56 of 65 show at least 85 SOL of inflow within 60 seconds, median exactly
85.0, which is the money the bonding curve needs to complete.

The curve-consistency filter, the other obvious suspect, is **innocent.** On the
original filter chain the band population is 42 coins with or without it, and the
filter only reads the first 60 seconds of candles while 84 of the 86 band coins
peak after that, so it cannot see the move being tested. (Two populations appear in
this section. **n=86 is the honest one** — every coin that crosses the band under a
rule you could actually run. n=42 is what was left after the earlier pass had also
deleted graduates and coins with quiet first minutes. The headline is n=86.)

**And then that correction was itself corrected, by measuring the one thing both
earlier passes had to assume.** The recheck above said graduates would need to give
back only about **9.60%** post-migration for band entry to be worse than buying
everything, and that nobody had ever captured the post-migration price. Somebody
has now: the giveback is **20.71% at the instant of migration by arithmetic, and
99.64% by twelve hours.** So the first pass had the direction right for the wrong
reason, the second pass had the method right and the answer wrong, and the thing
that decided it was a constant sitting in every pool-creation transaction the
whole time.

**That is the cleanest example in this document of the night's recurring lesson.**
Two careful passes argued about how to model an unmeasured quantity. Neither went
and measured it. It took one afternoon and it was exact.

The structural finding is unaffected and is the durable part: **the "middle" is not
a phase, it is a moment.** Median time from launch to $25k is 89 seconds (bounds
82–106s), and only 3 of 53 coins are still above $25k twelve hours later, with a
median 12-hour low back at launch. There is no consolidation base sitting there to
be traded.

**And the venue in the thesis is wrong.** Graduates do not migrate to Raydium.
They migrate to **PumpSwap** — 65 of 65 confirmed to have a real PumpSwap pool,
plus all 7 of flux's ground-truth `complete` events, with on-chain pool-creation
time matching the tracker's frozen peak to a median of −5 seconds. The
"consolidation" half of the stated thesis is no longer untestable and no longer
untested; it was measured above, on the right venue, and it loses by 5.7 points.

What remains true is the structural objection: a real band strategy scans the
standing inventory of coins already sitting at $25k–$80k, which **a launch listener
cannot see at all.** Seeding the universe from launches is the wrong instrument for
that question regardless of the answer.

**Two traps live in the data that will fool the next person who looks.** When a coin
migrates, the tracker keeps reporting its last curve price for 12 hours — `last ==
hi` for 20 of 20 such coins — which manufactures +820% returns on coins that did
nothing, and was the only source of a positive number anywhere in that analysis.
And survivorship: the same 53 coins bought at second 3 return **+75.10%** and
bought when they actually enter the band **−12.62%** — an 88-point swing from one
modelling choice, and the +75% is meaningless, because it is the return of the move
that *got them* to the band and you could not have bought it. (An earlier revision
paired the +75.10% with −62.06% and called that 88 points. It subtracts to 137, and
the −62.06% is the withdrawn graduates-deleted figure struck two paragraphs above.
Both are corrected here.)

## Holding for hours does not help either

Every exit rule this sprint tested was graded inside a 60-second window, because
the long-horizon data was believed unusable. It was not: `tracks-*.jsonl` runs to
about 12 hours and — verified on 4,083 of 4,083 joins — measures against the same
3-second entry price a strategy actually pays.

**State that carefully, because this one fact has now been got wrong twice tonight,
in opposite directions.** Both halves are needed. `tracks.hi`, `lo` and `cross` are
**reset to 1 when the tracker adopts a coin, so they do not cover the first 60
seconds** — `tracks.hi` is below the same coin's 60-second peak on **1,328 of
4,083** coins. What makes those ratios comparable to a 3-second entry is that the
tracker computes `mult = price / c.entry` against the original entry price, *not*
the `tracks.entry == outcome.entry` equality that keeps getting quoted on its own.
Anyone who reads `hi` and assumes it spans the coin's whole life will
under-report — and anyone who concludes from that under-reporting that the tracker
is broken will be wrong too. It is two windows on one ruler.

(A related trap for anyone re-running the hygiene: filtering on "price below the
curve floor" naively is a **4-decimal rounding artefact** — it flags 909 coins, of
which about 502 are noise.)

**Read everything in this section as in-sample, because it cannot be otherwise.**
The one genuine holdout in this corpus is 08-16, and
`tracks-2026-08-16.jsonl` holds **82 rows with a median `watchedSec` of 150
seconds, a maximum of 319, and not one row over an hour.** The 12-hour label on the
holdout is not thin — **it is absent.** **No twelve-hour exit result in this sprint
has an out-of-sample test anywhere in this data**, and that applies to every number
below.

Graded properly over the full 12 hours, **all 150 grid cells lose.** The best is
take-1.5x-stop-0.9 held to the end of tracking at **−3.38% net**, against −3.06%
for the *same rule cut at 60 seconds*. **Holding for hours costs 0.32 points and
ties up capital 500 times longer.** Bootstrap CI [−3.82%, −2.92%], P(profit) =
0.000, dropping the ten best coins makes it worse, and a 200-run shuffled null puts
best-of-grid at −5.5% and never once positive.

**The tail is real, sits in the later hours, and is about half the size it needs to
be.** 66% of 3x events and 86% of 5x events happen after second 60 — but only 1.7%
of coins ever reach 3x, and break-even on a 3x target needs 2.4%.

The obvious rule nobody had tried — sell at 60 seconds unless the coin is already
running, then hold — loses 1.2 to 3.4 points *more* than simply selling everything
at 60 seconds. **Coins that are up at 60 seconds do not stay up.**

That work also found the graduation freeze independently, from physics rather than
from the data's shape: the bonding curve completes at a virtual reserve of 115.0
SOL, so a recorded price sitting exactly at that ceiling means the coin has left
pump.fun and its `last` is frozen. 66 coins, worth a fake +3 points on the pooled
hold. Two passes reached that conclusion by different routes.

**This one has been rebuilt independently and it holds.** A second grader with its
own filter, path model and grid agrees to **0.01 points**: −3.39% against −3.38% on
the headline, −13.55% against −13.52% on hold-to-end, and every one of 30
held-to-end cells within 0.03.

**And excluding the graduated coins is innocent here**, which is worth one sentence
because deleting graduates is exactly what wrecked the band result three sections
above. The difference is selection. There, graduates were 19.8% of one arm and 1.6%
of the other, so removing them removed a fifth of one side's winners. Here every
coin is bought at second 3, so graduates are **1.95% of both sides**, and a 1.5x
take-profit exits them before they ever migrate. Put all 68 back under
sell-at-graduation and the headline moves to −3.31%, with **zero of 60 exactly
priced cells turning positive.** Those 68 are not corruption either: 46 of them
show `open.solIn = 85.0054 SOL` at second 3, meaning somebody bundled the entire
curve completion into the launch transaction, so entry *is* the graduation price
and a second-3 buy makes exactly 1.00x. For the doubters, the steelman is worth
stating and it does not help: all 68 would have to roughly **triple again on
PumpSwap** for even the worst rule in the grid to break even.

One correction to the horizon itself, in the same direction as everything else: the
later session's "12 hours" is really 0–10.2 hours, because the shutdown flush cuts
it. Restricting to coins actually watched 11 hours or more makes every number
**worse** (−3.79%, hold-to-end −16.83%).

**So exits are not where the missing edge is.** Every entry second has been tested
and every one is negative; every exit family has now been tested at both horizons
and every one is negative.

## There was never a clean era, and the biggest winner never existed

A quarter of the corpus — the days before candles were recorded — was excluded from
every headline on a technicality, and it looked **six points better** (−3.62%
against −9.85%). That was the last flattering slice in the dataset, and chasing it
closed the question rather than opening it.

**It is a duty-cycle artefact, not a regime.** On the only hours both eras actually
cover, the gap is **+0.3 points** (−10.97% versus −10.71%). 74% of the early data
sits in UTC 01–03, hours in which the later era had been connected for **one minute
in total**. Within-day hour swings are larger than the gap — one day runs −16.1% at
20:00 and −4.9% at 21:00 — session-level standard deviation is 6.6 points, and
deleting twenty coins from each era erases it. The market itself was not different:
about 1 SOL and 3 wallets per launch on every day compared.

The measurement was never different either: `outcome.entry` means the same thing on
all seven days (the curve price at the close of second 3), the producing code is
identical across every commit in that range, and if anything the *later* days buy
further into the run-up.

**And the corpus's single biggest winner is arithmetically impossible.** The 34.1x
had six wallets, 119 trades, and **17.7766 SOL in against 17.7766 SOL out — zero net
money** — while its recorded price claimed 149 SOL of inflow. **Ten of the top ten
peaks in the corpus fail the same test.**

Extending the curve-consistency check to the early days settles the last hope:
**31.4% fail there against 24.1% in the later era** — the days nobody could test are
the dirtier ones, and 21.2% of raw trades in the earliest raw log already carry a
reserve contradicting the curve. **There was never a clean era.**

The general lesson is the one the capture specification should carry in bold: **the
duty cycle is this dataset's biggest source of fake findings. No day-level or
era-level comparison should be believed until the same hours are captured on
different days.**

## The question that closes it: what would have to change?

Nothing about cost. **At zero fees the best rule still loses.**

A grid of realizable rules — entries at seconds 3, 5, 10, 20 and 30; trailing stops
at 5/10/20%; take-profit-and-stop pairs at 25/10, 50/20 and 100/30; and plain
hold-to-close — priced with realistic fills on 4,195 coins, tops out at
**−0.86% gross per trade** (enter at second 30, 5% trailing stop).

That is **before the pump.fun fee, before the network fee, before slippage, before
own-order impact.** Set every cost in this system to zero and it still loses.

So the things a person naturally reaches for next are all ruled out at once:

- Cheaper fees do not help — the loss exists at a fee of zero.
- A faster machine does not help — entering at second 3 is *worse* than second 30,
  and paying more does not buy an earlier slot.
- Jito bundles do not help — they buy position in a race whose winners return less
  than the wallets that never enter it.
- A larger bankroll does not help — the cost per trade is already negligible at
  0.05 SOL and above.

### The ceiling, measured properly, and what it changes

This document has always closed on a ceiling — what perfect foresight pays, as the
bound on everything below it. That number was **+18.9%** and it was wrong twice
over. Corrected, it is **+12.1%**.

Buy at exactly t = 3.0 seconds, sell at the highest price printed after it inside
the window, pay the 1.90%:

| population | ceiling |
|---|---|
| all coins, as recorded | +22.9% |
| **clean of the zero-fee actor (n=5,847)** | **+12.1%** |
| clean, over twelve hours | +20.0% |
| **the median coin, every row** | **−1.89%** |

Two things were wrong with +18.9%. It was struck a second late — see the note
below — and it was **contaminated**. The contamination is the more interesting
half: the **1,139 coins (16.3% of the 6,986 priced coins — the 17.3% quoted earlier is the same actor measured over the larger candle-judgeable corpus of 7,971, so both are right) that actor touched carry a ceiling of +78.6%
against +12.1% for everything else.** Sixteen percent of the corpus supplied
**53% of the entire uncleaned ceiling.** Three independent routes agree, including
flux's raw events on different days (+12.60% against +12.07%).

**And the median row says the whole thing in one line: the median launch never
trades above its 3-second price again.** A ceiling of −1.89% means that even a
crystal ball, on the typical coin, just pays the fee.

**A note on the entry second, so nobody re-opens it.** `outcome.entry` is the close
of the last candle with `s ≤ 2`, on 99.3–99.7% of coins per day. Candle `s` is the
bucket *start*, so that close is the price at exactly **t = 3.0 s**, which is
precisely where `open.seconds == 3` puts the features. **The sprint's entry is
right and nobody is early.** One report alone is a second late: its "last price at
or before second 3" means `s ≤ 3`, i.e. t = 4.0 s — confirmed by its own sample
size, since an `s ≤ 3` filter gives exactly its n=6,989. That costs about 1.7
points of ceiling per second, which is why it bit the ceiling and barely touched
that report's other numbers.

### The one thing that passed every test, and what it turned out to be

Selecting on **decision-time features only** produced the single statistically
strongest predictive result of the sprint, and it is worth recording how hard it
was tested before saying what it is.

| test | result | independent rebuild |
|---|---|---|
| leave-one-session-out | −8.35% → **−3.43%**, 4 of 4 sessions | −8.41% → −3.65%, 4 of 4 |
| **a four-day, different-process holdout** — fit on 08-20/08-21, score on 08-16 across 3.78 days of silence and five distinct recorder processes | **−7.09% → −3.77%** | the `s0` fold, promoted |
| fit on the past, score the future | −6.64% → **−2.60%** | −6.64% → −1.84% |
| shuffled-label null | **99.5th percentile** globally, 100th within session, null median −0.12 | reproduced |

**It is not a split artefact**, and it was rebuilt from scratch on an
independently constructed grid (1,046 cells against 1,109) with the same universe,
the same ceiling (+12.07%) and the same baseline (−8.41%). Nothing else in this
document survived that much checking.

**One fold in that table is stronger than it looks, and two caveats go with it.**
The `s0` fold is 08-16, fitted only on 08-20 and 08-21 — which is not merely
leave-one-out but a **genuine holdout in time**: 3.78 days of total silence and
five distinct recorder processes separate them (see *What the data can and cannot
settle*). So this negative result **survives a four-day, different-process
holdout** on the cleanest capture in the corpus. It is *not* an independent
population — 81.2% of 08-16's clean coins share a wallet with the fitting days —
so the claim is stability over time, not generalisation. The rules involved read
only `open.*`, `initialBuySol` and `social.kind`, so the 4.3% `funding` coverage on
08-16 does not bite here. **The 08-21 version of it should not be quoted**,
and is struck: 08-21 is burst-truncated on 46.8% of its clean coins against 7.7% on
08-16, and hours 01–08 hold three usable coins, so a finding whose whole mechanism
is *"it selects coins that never trade again"* was being validated on the one day
whose dead population is inflated by the recorder.

**The result reproduces on the real holdout, and its significance does not.**
Scored on 08-16 having been fitted only on 08-20 + 08-21, it gives **−7.11% →
−3.77%**, matching the `s0` fold to two decimals, and the mechanism comes with it:
**53.9% of the selected coins have zero candles against a 24.2% base rate, and 58%
return exactly −1.890%.** So the *effect* is genuinely out of sample.

**But shuffle within exposure strata — compare each coin only against others that
traded about as much — and it is a coin flip.** In sample that test put it at the
92.5th percentile. **On the holdout the null median lift is +3.03pp against an
observed +3.34: the 58th percentile.** So the two statements have to travel
together, and this document makes both: **−7.11% → −3.77% is a genuine
out-of-sample improvement, and 91% of it is "this coin will never trade again".**
It is an exposure detector with nothing else in it.

**And it is a dead-coin detector.** What it selects has a median of **1 buyer, 1
trade and 0.01 SOL**; a median of **zero candles after entry**; 70% of its picks
have completely flat paths; and **69% of them return exactly −1.89%, the fee and
nothing else.** The improvement is not finding winners. It is buying silence — on a
bonding curve a coin nobody trades hands your money back minus the round trip,
which beats the average because the average is a coin that falls.

**The mechanism is confirmed twice, by moving the dial in both directions.**
Tighten the selection and the dead share and the return move together: at one bar
it keeps 1,296 coins at −2.73% with **71% having no candles at all after entry**.
Demand instead that the fitted mean be positive — that is, ask it to find *winners*
— and the dead share falls to 5% and the result collapses to **−8.83%**. **Every
point of improvement is bought with a dead coin.** And they are genuinely dead
rather than a recording gap: **98.9% have `total.trades == open.trades`**, checked
from the wallet side, so nothing was missed after the window.

**This also corrects the ceiling result above, where two opposite rules were read
as one.** They are **independent** — 164 coins overlap where 170 would be expected
by chance, so knowing a coin is in one tells you nothing about the other — and they
point in opposite directions:

| what the selection does | share of coins | profile | ceiling | actual hold |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| lifts the **ceiling** | 11% | ~11 early buyers, 13 SOL | **+36.3%** | **−15.3%** |
| lifts the **outcome** | 29% | the dead ones | +3.0% | **−3.7%** |
| *both at once* | 164 coins | behaves like a ceiling coin | +35.1% | **−13.4%** |

So "selection lifts both the ceiling and the real outcome" is wrong. **Two
different selections lift two different things**, the one that finds coins with
something in them is the one that loses most, and **the overlap behaves like a
ceiling coin — so combining them buys the bad half.**

**The asymptote is minus the fee**, and it is now measured against a real holdout
rather than a day boundary. Best of 13 exit rules under selection: **+0.09% gross,
−1.81% net.** *Not trading beats it by 1.8 points.* Pushed any
closer to break-even it dies the standard death of this sprint, and it dies harder
in the rebuild than it did the first time: the median trade is **−22.3%**, dropping
one coin takes it to **−7.71%**, and **3 of 4 sessions are negative.**

**And the epitaph, which needs no number.** The thing that would make this work —
knowing whether a coin is still alive a few seconds in — is not available at the
moment you have to commit. (An earlier version of this paragraph put a figure on
that, from coins that traded for at least ten seconds. **Do not quote it**: it
swings from −8.49% to +12.69% on a protocol choice at n≈50. The point stands
without it, and it is the same point the exposure-stratified null makes above.)

So the record should not say "there is nothing there" — that was never available on
this evidence — and it should not say the result failed validation, because it did
not. It should say the plain thing: **the one apparent improvement was not a split
artefact. It was a dead-coin detector.**

One thing that closes with it: **going from a 60-second window to 300 seconds
raises the clean ceiling by one point.** "Extend the capture window and re-ask" has
been suggested repeatedly all sprint. It is not the cheap re-ask it looks like.

**Caveats.** The +12.1% is an **upper bound** — measuring corruption beyond that
one actor needs per-trade reserves, which this corpus does not have. Landing fee
and own-order impact are excluded throughout. The twelve-hour figure leans on
`tracks.hi`, which could not be audited.

## Wall 2 — every signal detects volatility, not direction

This is now the entire case, and it is the one nothing has moved.

The signals are **real**. They are not noise and not overfitting:

- Tweet reuse sorts outcomes 14.4% → 0.5% across reuse buckets. Monotone, 30x
  spread, replicates out of sample.
- "A wallet we have seen buy early three times before just bought this" moves the
  2x rate from 0.40% to 9.39% on unseen data, and survived four attempts to kill it.
- Order flow predicts the peak at 10 to 15 standard deviations.

And every one is worth nothing, for the same reason. **Order flow predicts the peak
at z = +10 to +15 and predicts the finish equally strongly in the opposite
direction.** The coins with the most early sellers have the highest peak (1.68x)
and a losing close (0.88x). The best social bucket returns −12.3% while the worst
returns −6.6%. The wallet-graph slice returns −3.92% where taking everything
returns −3.87%.

Selecting for "this one sometimes pops" is selecting for "this one usually
craters". They are the same coins. It is a very good volatility detector pointed at
a population that drifts down, and there is no way to be short.

**We can tell which coins will move**, and that is measured rather than asserted:
a second-3 selection lifts the perfect-foresight ceiling from +12.1% to **+34.2%**.
**We cannot tell which way, we cannot tell when to leave, and the one selection
that reliably improves the real outcome does it by picking coins where nothing
happens at all** — median 1 buyer, 1 trade, 0.01 SOL, and 69% of them returning
exactly the fee. Detecting a coin that will move and detecting a coin that will pay
are two different problems, and this project only ever solved the first.

A mechanism for the downward drift was found: **about 70% of creators dump their
own coin inside the first minute** — 71.3% across 3,835 coins with outcome labels,
66.6% getting all the way out across 5,241 curve-verified launches — **and they
take a median 13% over their own buy, which is +39.2% on the money they staked.**
(An earlier revision said "a median 5.2 seconds in, about 7%". That came from a
226-creator sample on two days that carry no outcome labels at all. Use the large
samples.)

## Wall 3 — STRUCK. The bankroll was never the problem.

Revision 1 said a launch-slot landing costs 1,005,000 lamports, so a €200 bankroll
could not pay the toll. **That was wrong, and the error is instructive.**

1,005,000 is the **p90 of the whole fee distribution** — and it is a round number
because it is the "high priority" preset in retail sniper front-ends. The original
pass drew 25 samples and hit a UI default. With thousands of enriched
transactions: the median fee across all trades is 55,000 lamports, the slot-0
median is **185,000** on the largest sample (n=769; a smaller earlier pass got
205,000, and the retired-numbers table records both), and
**being the first buyer costs about what being the fifty-first costs** (53,000 vs
55,000).

For the population we would actually belong to — late buyers — the network fee is a
**median 0.107% of the SOL bought.** Effective cost is 0.00012–0.00032 SOL per
landed trade. **Minimum viable order is 0.026–0.063 SOL**, not the 0.25–0.4 SOL
revision 1 claimed. A round trip at 0.1 SOL is 2.2–2.6% all in, and pump.fun's flat
fee is the bulk of it.

One late scare nearly rebuilt this wall at 15x: **93.2% of pump.fun transactions
fail on-chain**, and a failed Solana transaction still pays its fee. **That number
was wrong by 274x, it was ours, and the correction was sitting in the archive
unlooked-at.**

Counted directly — `meta.err` over **whole blocks**, not over a subscription —
the real rate is **12,429 failures in 109,883 pump transactions across seven
windows from October 2024 to August 2026: 11.3%, range 6.6–22.6%.** In the same
session the listener's *successes* match a full-block scan to within 7% (54.7/s
against 51.0/s) while its *failures* run **1,426/s against a true 5.2/s**. So the
listener's failure counter is broken; the chain is fine.

**And it is not the bug this document previously blamed.** Revision 3 said the
`err` check sits before the dedup in `listen.js`, so redelivered failures
double-count. Replay is measurably absent — **0 of 11,054 signatures were ever
delivered twice** — and double counting cannot produce a 274x error in any case.
That explanation was rightly withdrawn.

**The real mechanism is now measured, from the opposite direction.** A block sweep
finds `getSignaturesForAddress` returning **142.9 signatures per block** against the
**~17** that log-matching finds. **The gap is 126 transactions a block that *name*
the pump program but die before its code ever runs — 88.1%.** `logsSubscribe` with
`mentions` matches on **account keys**, so it sees every transaction that names the
program, and 88% of those never produce a pump.fun event at all. **The listener
counted them as pump.fun failures.**

So the two numbers are both real and they count different populations. **93.2% is
the share of transactions that mention the program and fail — mostly before
reaching it. 11.3% is the share that actually reached the program and errored.**
The listener was counting the first and reporting it as the second. That is the
whole of it, and the hedge this paragraph used to carry can come off.

**The physics argument that used to sit here was inverted, and it is gone.** The
arithmetic was right — 12M CU per account per block ÷ 88,935 CU a trade = 135
transactions per block on one curve — but the cap is **never approached**: the most
ever observed is 28 landed trades on a coin-slot, 29% of it. Failures count against
that same cap, so ~1,700 failures on one curve would need at least thirteen fully
saturated blocks. **The cap forbids the compression it was invoked to explain.**
There is no launch-slot stampede: the mean launch draws **1.44** non-dev slot-0
buys, and **62% of launches draw none at all.**

**So Wall 3 is now a census, not an inference.** Attempts per fill: **1.13**.
Effective round trip: **135,000 lamports — 0.27% of a 0.05 SOL order, 0.14% of a
0.1 SOL order**, rising to about 1.2% only if both legs pay p75 fees. Twenty-two
months of whole blocks say the same thing.

**Do not let this argument come back.** It has now been wrong three times, in both
directions, and every time the error was in our own instrument rather than in the
market. We are cheap to trade and too slow to trade. The second is fatal alone.

## What the code is missing

**Start here, because it reframes the rest of this section: nothing in the Rust
crate has ever read a real capture.** No file under `src-tauri/` opens
`coins-*.jsonl`, `tracks-*.jsonl` or `tweets-*.jsonl`. There is not one
`sts.replay.v1` fixture anywhere on disk. `fixtures.rs` — a *synthetic* fixture
generator, which is the one thing this project's own first rule forbids — opens
with the words "launches that never happened".

So **"1,654 tests pass" means the engine agrees with itself.** Every number in this
document came from Python written during the sprint, against the raw files, going
around the engine entirely. That is also why the three absences below went
unnoticed for months: nothing ever ran end to end against real data, so nothing
surfaced them.

1,500 KB of Rust. Execution state machines, Jito bundle support, geyser pipelines,
24-hop forensic tracing — and read closely, several of those are not what the words
say:

- **There is no Jito client.** No HTTP, no gRPC, no endpoint; the crate has no HTTP
  dependency at all. What exists is a tip-floor function you must feed by hand and
  a retention timer over bundle *ID strings*. `BundleRecord` contains no
  transactions. Nothing is ever bundled.
- **`execution.rs` could not produce a transaction a validator would accept.**
  There is no ed25519 anywhere in the crate — the mock signer returns "sixty-four
  bytes over the message, not a signature; a receipt shaped like one" — and there
  is no PDA derivation, so the sell's account list is built out of label hashes.
  The message compiler, the account ordering and the `global:sell` discriminator
  are real and correct. The parts that make it a transaction are absent.
- **The MEV simulator says so itself**, at the top of 3,387 lines: *"Nothing in
  here is a measurement."* Every constant in it declares that it was invented. That
  is good practice, and it is also the answer to anyone who quotes one.
- **"24-hop tracing" is a depth counter.** The test that proves 24 walks a
  single-file chain of 24 nodes with one edge each. On real branching data, at the
  default node budget, it truncates at two or three hops.
- **The UI suite does not drive the real window.** It serves `ui/` over plain
  localhost HTTP, spawns Chrome, and replaces the Tauri bridge with a hand-written
  JavaScript object. No Rust runs. And Tauri on macOS renders in WKWebView while
  those pixel assertions go through Blink, so they measure an engine the product
  does not ship.

The three things a trading system needs are the three that do not exist:

1. **No exit rule.** Nowhere in `src-tauri/src/` is there a stop, a target, a
   trailing stop or a time exit. The daemon's only exit is flatten-at-end. The
   backtest harness does not compute exits — it prices exit events handed to it.
2. **No paper mode.** `OperatingMode::Paper` is never constructed outside
   `#[cfg(test)]`. Not gated off; never written. This is why `paper_trades` is empty.
3. **No entry-side transaction builder.**

**The gates were never enforced, at any phase — until tonight.** Phase 0
criterion 1 requires `cargo clippy --workspace --all-targets -- -D warnings` and
`cargo fmt --check` to pass. Neither ever had. The true warning count was **62**
(52 lib + 1 bin + 9 across two integration files), and `cargo fmt` had never been
run at all — 1,576 hunks across all 44 Rust files. **Both now pass, at `fda41e8`,
with 1,654 tests green and no new lint suppressions.** Phase 1 criterion 4
("critical facts require two consistent providers or are UNKNOWN") was never
implemented at all — and no second provider is configured, so it could not run if
it were. Phase 3's gate is the one this document closes.

Worth noting how the clippy number hid: `-D warnings` promotes warnings to errors,
so cargo stops at the first failing unit and reports only that unit's count. The
figure everyone quoted was one target's total mistaken for the workspace's.

Freeze the forensics *for trading*. It improves ranking in the middle, which we
never trade, and makes the top 5% *worse* (19.28% → 15.66%). The path-scoring in
`tracer.rs` is a separate matter and is worth keeping on its own merits: it is real
forensics, byte-deterministic, and it answers a question people are paid to answer.

**And the engineering is not what failed here.** `replay.rs` — 6,357 lines, 123
tests, not one import from the rest of the crate — holds `CurveState`: integer-exact
constant-product arithmetic with the fee taken outside the curve and rounding always
against the trader. **It is the only component in this project that has ever been
checked against reality, and it passed.** It predicts `initialBuyTokens` from
`initialBuySol` exactly on 5,927 of 5,959 real trades, and inverted on 35,619 real
round-trip wallets it puts 97.0% at a curve position that is physically possible.
Beside it sits a hash-chained record format whose cursor cannot seek, which turns
"this decision could not have seen a later record" into a property of the type
rather than of the reviewer's attention.

Worth saying plainly, because it is the sharpest thing this sprint learned about
its own conduct: **the catalogue of errors in this document — a p90 read as a
median, stops priced at the stop level, deleted graduates, frozen migrated prices,
a table with the awkward row removed — was made outside that file, in scripts that
hurriedly reimplemented what it already had right.** The engine was never the
problem. The analysis would have been better if it had gone through the engine
instead of around it. That, and not the loss per trade, is the part worth carrying
to whatever comes next.

## What the data can and cannot settle

**08-21 is not a holdout. A real one exists and it is 08-16 — and this makes one
negative result more credible rather than making anything more favourable.**

Two passes measured the 08-20/08-21 boundary and reported gaps ninety minutes
apart. Both were right. **The discrepancy is one coin.** RAMEN
(`Cuw4UoDxoPm1…`), launched 08-20 23:43:02.653, is simultaneously the **last row of
`coins-2026-08-20.jsonl`** and the **first coin of the session** that runs into
08-21. The gap *before* it is **5,995.4 s (99.92 min)**; the gap *after* it is
**611.2 s**. One pass measured the leading edge, the other the trailing edge, of
the same launch. It is not a filtering artefact either: rebuilding the split raw
and filtered gives byte-identical boundaries.

**And there is a third boundary, which is the honest one.** The 08-21 file does not
begin on 08-21 — five rows at the top of it launched on **08-20 at 23:53:13–16**,
and six rows in that file belong to 08-20 in total. Measured on launch time, **the
true gap at the day line is 478.9 s: seven minutes and 59 seconds.**

**And the cleanest way to retire the question is one sentence about where the gap
sits.** The 100-minute break is at **08-20 22:03 → 23:43 — two hours before
midnight.** The gap at the 08-20/08-21 boundary is **eight minutes, smaller than
twenty separate gaps inside 08-21 itself.** Both passes found the same gap; one
placed it on the wrong side of a day.

The cause is mechanical and is a recorder defect worth one line: **files rotate on
write time, not launch time.** Median write lag is 1.00 minute, p99 is **17.06**,
so a deferred flush wrote those 23:53 launches at 00:01:10. **Only 5 of the 7 files
begin at a session boundary at all.**

The listener's own records close it without inferring anything from launch gaps.
**pid 11679 ran 08-20 18:54:42 → 08-21 09:53:43 — 899 minutes, 4,921 coins, 70
reconnects, ending on an explicit `stop`.** Seven pids exist in the corpus, each
contiguous, never interleaving. Three details make it airtight:

- pid 11679's **only byte-offset-0 row is its opening connect.** A restart writes
  another; there is no other.
- **The offset counter carries straight across the file rotation.** The first
  08-21 audit row carries offset **1,404,096 — exactly the 08-20 audit file's final
  size on disk.** A process started after midnight could not know that number.
- **The listener logged the outage itself**: a `gap` row at 23:43:01 with
  `ms = 5,993,856` (99.90 min) and `from = 22:03:07.623` — a timestamp it could
  only have been holding in memory.

"Unbroken" overstates it — 70 disconnect/reconnect cycles are logged inside that
run — but it is one process, heavily duty-cycled, and 611 seconds is far under the
60-minute session threshold. **08-21 is one session with 08-20, and its total
active capture is 48.3 minutes across ten hours**, which confirms the phrase used
elsewhere in this document to the tenth of a minute.

**And the same defect is visible from inside the program.**
`tracks-2026-08-20.jsonl` ends at 23:53:16 while `tracks-2026-08-21.jsonl` starts
at 00:01:15 — **the tracker assigned those five coins to 08-20 and the coins
recorder did not.** Two components of the same program disagreed about which day a
coin belonged to, and the disagreement sat in the corpus unnoticed for the whole
sprint.

**So the favourable correction is not there, and this document should not claim
one.** 08-21 is not a holdout and **nothing is promoted on that basis.** What is
true is narrower: **"there is no valid held-out day" is false.** **08-16
qualifies** — it sits behind **5,445.7 minutes, 3.78 days, of total silence** (no
files of any kind exist for 08-17, 08-18 or 08-19), with five distinct recorder
processes on one side and two on the other, and it yields **1,505 clean coins,
1,389 untruncated**, at 7.7% truncation and a 19.8% dead-coin rate. Fit on 08-20 +
08-21, score on 08-16.

**State the limit with it, every time, because it is a real limit and not a
footnote: 08-16 is a holdout in time, not in population.** **81.2% of its clean
coins share a wallet with 08-20/08-21**, 23.3% share a creator, the manufacturing
actor operates on both sides, and the median `open.solIn` moved 0.60 → 2.67 SOL
between them. So the right phrase is **"survives a four-day, different-process
holdout"**, never "survives an independent sample". It tests stability over time.
It is still the only such test this corpus supports, and it is worth a great deal.

**One hard limit on what can be scored there at all.** The `funding` block covers
**79 of 1,822 coins on 08-16 — 4.3%**, against 77% and 87% on 08-20/08-21, and all
79 come from a single five-minute run. **No wallet-graph or funder feature can be
held out on 08-16.** The same is true of `tracks`: all 82 rows come from that same
five-minute window, so 08-16's "4.5% track coverage" is one slice rather than a
sample. The dead-coin selection result is unaffected — its rules read only
`open.*`, `initialBuySol` and `social.kind` — and that is worth saying out loud so
nobody later assumes the wallet-graph findings were held out too. **They were
not.**

**And that test has now been run properly**, fitting on 08-20 + 08-21 and scoring
08-16 once. What it settles:

- **The base rate survives.** −10.41% ±4.18 net on the clean set (n=1,505), and
  **−7.10% ±2.96** untruncated with the manufacturing actor removed. The central
  negative result of this sprint holds on a genuine holdout.
- **Flow exits survive**, at the 100th percentile of their own grid null on both
  blocks, with train-chosen rules transferring without any re-search.
- **The dead-coin selection reproduces** at −7.11% → −3.77%, matching the `s0`
  fold to two decimals.
- **Two significance claims do not survive** — the selection result's and the sell
  side's. Both are downgraded where they appear.
- **And no twelve-hour result can be tested at all**, because
  `tracks-2026-08-16.jsonl` has 82 rows, median `watchedSec` 150 seconds, none over
  an hour. **The 12-hour label on the holdout is absent, not thin.**

**Which is the right shape for an ending: the negative result got stronger and the
positive ones got weaker** — what has happened every time anything in this sprint
was recomputed.

**Read the shape of that carefully, because it is the only correction all night
that could have gone the other way.** No finding becomes more favourable. **One
negative finding becomes more credible.** The go/no-go does not move.

**08-21 is disqualified twice over**, and this belongs beside the dead-coin result
specifically: **46.8% of its clean coins are burst-truncated** against **7.7%** on
08-16, and **hours 01–08 contain three usable coins.** A finding whose entire
mechanism is *"it selects coins that never trade again"* was being scored on the
one day whose dead population is inflated by the recorder.

> **The analysis rule this produces, and it should be applied everywhere:
> assign every coin by its launch timestamp `t`, never by filename.**

Two cheap recorder fixes follow from it: **rotate files on launch `t`**, and
**record actual watched seconds instead of a hardcoded `follow: 60`** (the second
is already done in `tools/capture`; the first is not).
The capture is duty-cycled — median gap between launches is 0.8 seconds, so the 52
multi-minute gaps are outages, not quiet markets — and 14% of outcomes are silently
truncated when the listener stops.

**But the recorder does not drop launches, and the claim that it dropped nine in
ten was wrong.** Checked against the chain rather than inferred from inside the
capture — sweeping real blocks and comparing launch for launch — flux caught **62
of 62**, and the STS recorder on its busiest recorded minute caught **75 of 75**:
`skipped: 0, missing: 0`, **137 of 137, zero drops.** The captures are essentially
**complete while connected. They are simply short.**

**And one of this sprint's own hygiene rules was wrong, which is the night's lesson
landing on the people who were applying it.** Every pass dropped rows
where `len(outcome.highs) >= 60`, on the belief that a full array meant a truncated
or frozen record. **There was no defect to filter for.** `outcome.highs` is a hard
array cap — 69 coins sit at exactly 60 and **none above** (`lows` caps at 29) — and
the running extreme kept moving after the array stopped. The clean demonstration is
this: on **65 of the 69**, `peakAtSec` is *later* than the last second the array
records — median second **51 against 32.1** — and `highs[-1]` disagrees with
`peakMult` on **66 of 69**. **Had the cap frozen the running extreme, those two
would be equal.** Nothing was frozen. On 08-10 the field is never written at all,
so the rule is a silent no-op there; 08-11 and 08-12 do write it, 66 and 34 rows,
none of them capped.

What it did instead was delete winners: **69 coins, none of them the actor's, at a
median peak of 3.14x — half of every genuine 5x coin in the corpus.** Compared
like for like, on the four days the field exists, it removes **1.16% of base rows
and 52.9% of the real-tail coins: a 46x enrichment.** (An earlier version of this
paragraph paired 0.86% of rows against 52.9% of the tail. Those are different
populations — seven days against four — and the like-for-like figure is both
honest and worse.) **A hygiene rule that nobody re-derived, applied by forty
passes, quietly deleted the most interesting coins in the dataset.**

**Two of its effects are not conservative, and the second one matters.**

1. **The confidence interval is 29% too narrow.** ±0.86 becomes **±1.11** points
   once the deleted rows are back. This document argues in places from a ±3.1-point
   resolution; that number was measured on the thinned corpus and should be read as
   a floor rather than a figure.
2. **It inflated the measured value of exiting early by about two points.** The
   best early exit beats hold-to-end by +8.85 points under the filter and **+6.70
   points** without it — because the coins the rule deleted are precisely the ones
   *still climbing at second 50*. That is about a quarter of the whole gap to
   break-even. **Both exit grids have since been re-run without it**, and the
   prediction held: on the flow grid the same statistic moves **+9.80 → +7.93
   points**, against a forecast 2.15-point inflation.
3. **It deleted asymmetrically across arms in a second, independent analysis.**
   Both band analyses used it, and there it removes **0.91% of the baseline against
   15.7% of the band arm — 17x.** That is the same shape as the graduate deletion
   that wrecked the first band result, arrived at independently, which is what
   makes the problem structural rather than a one-off: **the same filter produced
   asymmetric deletion in two unrelated analyses, and the drop-test catches exactly
   that.**
4. **And it suppressed a real signal, which is the part nobody predicted.** With
   the deleted coins back, the flow grid moves from the **0th percentile of its own
   null to the 100th**. A filter that removes the biggest movers does not merely
   shrink an effect; it can reverse the test that decides whether the effect is
   there at all. (The 0th percentile turned out to be a *broken* null as well as a
   filtered one — see *Neither lead reaches break-even* below, where both faults
   are separated.)

**The corrected recipe, for anyone grading this corpus again:** keep
`follow == 60`, the 60-second burst guard, non-null outcome fields, the actor
exclusion, and candle-based curve consistency. **Delete `len(highs) >= 60` except
where a method literally walks the arrays** — which is where the rule was born, and
where it is correct. It was over-generalised into a universal instruction.

**And the one-minute diagnostic that would have caught it**, worth more than the
rule it corrects: **cross-tabulate what any filter drops against the peak multiple.
If the dropped rows have a higher mean peak than the kept rows — 3.58 against 1.24
here — it is a selection rule, not hygiene.**

**Applied to ourselves, because a rule you only recommend is not a rule.** The
actor filter — the one this document leans on hardest — **also fails that test**:
the coins it drops have a mean peak of **1.918** against **1.159** for the coins it
keeps. It is still justified, but *not* by the drop-test, which it fails. It is
justified by outside evidence: **none of the 86 real-tail coins is actor-touched**,
so what it removes is manufactured rather than merely large. That is exactly the
standard the array-cap rule could not meet, and the difference between the two is
the whole lesson.

**The sharpest statement of the sampling problem is not a percentage.** It is
this: **08-21 is not a day. It is 48 minutes spread over ten hours.** Everything
this document says about a "day" is a statement about a handful of connected
minutes scattered through it, at hours that differ from every other day's. **See
*There was never a clean era* above for what that does to any comparison between
days** — it is this dataset's biggest single source of fake findings and it
produced two of them this sprint.

But it is not hopeless. **Comparing a filter against buy-everything within the same
session** cancels the session effect, and those paired comparisons have a spread of
only 1.2–3.8 points. A 3-point edge **is** resolvable with the six sessions already
on disk, at 150+ coins per cell.

## Neither lead reaches break-even — but "closed" was too strong

Two candidates survived long enough to be called paths to break-even. **Neither
gets there.** But both grids were graded under the array-cap filter described in
*What the data can and cannot settle*, and re-running them without it **restored 92
coins to the flow grid and 56 to the sell-side grid** — none actor-touched, median
peak **2.32x**, 37 of the 92 above 3x. Those were genuine large movers that a bad
hygiene rule had deleted, and putting them back moves two conclusions and inverts a
third.

**The headline is unchanged: no go.** What changes is that flow exits are not
worthless. They are a real effect that does not reach far enough, which is both
more accurate and more interesting than the sentence this section used to carry.

1. **Flow-based exits — real, and still short.** The original 1.8-point figure was
   a *holding-time* effect and that correction stands: it compared a flow rule
   exiting at 3.9 seconds against a price stop sitting in the trade for 32.7. But
   two sentences that followed it are **withdrawn**, and they were quoted
   prominently:

   - ~~"flow versus a plain 5-second stopwatch is −0.06 points"~~ → paired
     properly it is **+0.75 points [+0.23, +1.37]**, and the range across every
     sensible matching is **+0.69 to +1.13**. Two details make the reversal exact
     rather than a judgement call: the original sentence compared a **3.9-second
     rule against a 5-second clock**, so matched properly it was **−0.55pp on its
     own data — worse than it was published as**; and the best flow rule already
     beat a matched clock by **+0.72pp with the array cap still on.** **The effect
     was there before the filter was lifted. The filter only hid the null.**
   - ~~"the whole 100-rule grid sits at the 0th percentile of its own null"~~ → it
     sits at the **100th percentile**, confirmed three separate ways (the original
     108-rule grid, the 100-rule grid, and with clocks stripped from both sides),
     best rule +0.62% against a null p95 of **+0.06%**. This is **a data change,
     not a method change.**

   **But the 0th percentile was never a measurement, and the real explanation is
   more useful than the one this document recorded.** That null grid **contained a
   plain 3-second stopwatch**, and a stopwatch is unchanged by shuffling exit
   seconds across coins. On that corpus the stopwatch *was* the best rule — so the
   shuffled best **could not fall below the true best**, and 46% of null draws sit
   pinned exactly on it. Like-for-like, that corpus is at the **45th percentile,
   not the 0th**. We recorded it as a data effect; **it was a broken null.**

   > **A null that contains a rule the shuffle cannot touch has a floor at the
   > observed value. Check that before quoting a percentile.**

   **Flow genuinely times exits.** Best flow exit: **+0.62% gross, −1.50% net** —
   up from −2.28% net, and still **1.5 points short of break-even**.

   **And the +0.62% is not credible even so.** Its interval is **±0.73**, five
   coins carry it, dropping the top five leaves **−0.00%**, one second of reaction
   takes it to +0.04% and two seconds to **−0.56%**, and it loses on session 4. So
   the *effect* is real and the *level* is not bankable — a different statement
   from the one this document made before, and the difference matters for what
   anyone builds next.

2. **The many-hands filter — dead, and it earned the kill.** "Ten or more buys in
   3 seconds, price still within 40% of the floor" reproduced exactly, at +16.97%
   gross on n=349 against a −7.12% baseline. It passed both traps that killed
   everything else: matched against a constant 20-second exposure it is still
   **+16.95 points** ahead, so it is not the clock in disguise, and it holds to the
   window close with no stop, so the fill correction has nothing to bite on. It
   died five other ways.
   - **It was never "many hands."** The filter counts *buys*, not buyers. On
     distinct wallets, ≥10 gives +10.7% and **≥15 gives −2.3%** — more hands is
     worse past twelve. A wider search's single best cell was "fewer than 15
     wallets AND 22 or more trades": few hands, many clicks, the opposite of the
     story the filter was named for.
   - **The hands are a bot fleet.** The median first-3s buyer inside the filter
     appears in **52 other launches**, and 80 for the twenty best coins.
   - **One coin is 52% of all the profit.** Drop 3 coins and it is −0.31%; drop 5
     and it is −4.76%. Every cell on the surrounding parameter surface has a
     negative median.
   - **It inverts out of sample.** On the largest genuinely separate session
     (08-11, n=1,966) it returns **−11.60% against −0.99% for buying everything**,
     with a confidence interval excluding zero.
   - **87th percentile of pure noise**, over 5,263 filters re-searched on 60
     shuffles.

   Combining it with a flow exit gives no synergy, for a reason worth keeping: the
   filter is a bet on the tail and a flow exit is a machine for cutting tails off.
   They are opposed by construction. The only honest residue is that filtered coins
   reach 3x inside a minute at twice the base rate (5.73% against 2.78%) — one cell
   out of 5,263, and no exit rule turns it into money.

**And no filter survives the real holdout either.** Choosing cells on 08-20 +
08-21 and scoring them once on 08-16, three appear to clear — the best at **+4.15%
gross / +2.18% net on n=105** — and all three die the same three deaths: **gone at
drop-top-3, gone at one second of latency, 87th percentile of a null that redoes
the whole selection pipeline, and a median trade of exactly +0.00%.** That last
number is the tell, and it is the same tell as everywhere else in this document.

**Nothing under test reaches break-even.** That is the accurate version of what
this section used to say. Flow exits and the sell side both carry real, repeatable
information about *when* to leave; neither carries enough of it.

Also worth keeping, though none of it makes anything profitable: the wallet graph
as a *reject* filter (drops 47% of launches losing only 3 of 65 winners);
and excluding coins on a reused or stale tweet, half of which never reach a
tradeable price.

**`open.sellers` has been withdrawn from this list.** An earlier pass reported it
lifting the 1.5x rate from 4.7% to 16.3%. A dedicated sell-side investigation found
that **does not reproduce — it inverts** (2.86% with no early sellers versus 7.51%
with them, on the 12-hour labels), and that the apparent effect is the crowd-size
signal in disguise: it flips sign once buy count is held fixed.

**What the sell side contains is real, and it is the strongest surviving signal in
the sprint.** "Exit on the first sell" beats a stopwatch held the same average time
by **+3.64 points** — down from the +5.13 reported under the array-cap filter, so
about **70% of the claimed effect survives**, and the level is now what matters.
It stands up to everything asked of it: the **100th percentile of 300 shuffled-exit
nulls**, **+3.18 points** after a two-second reaction lag, the same sign in all
three sessions (+4.86 / +2.87 / +1.65), and **+4.39 points out of sample on
08-16**. **And the edge grows when winners are deleted**, so it is not a tail
artefact. Three passes found independently that sells mark the turn; with the two
grids re-run they now agree on the *size* of it rather than differing by two
points.

**The level is where it fails.** Re-run, the best of 240 sell-side
filter-and-exit combinations goes from +0.43% gross / −1.47% net to **+3.66% gross
/ +1.76% net** — which would be the first cell anywhere in this sprint above
break-even. **It is struck.** It was examined specifically because it would
otherwise become the most quoted line in this document, and it dies five ways:

- **Two coins decide whether it clears.** Winsorising any single coin at +100%
  gives +1.34%; at +50% it gives **−1.02%**.
- **The median member loses 1.36%.**
- **Its own null clears break-even 3.7% of the time.** A cell at +3.66% inside that
  null is not a finding. (Interval ±4.35 points; session 3 is −4.14%.)
- **On 112 of its 359 coins no trade printed in the second we mark the exit.**
  Those average **−4.59%**, against +7.40% for the coins where one did. The result
  lives in seconds where the fill is imputed rather than observed.
- **It has no sell-side content in its exit whatsoever**, and the confirmation is
  total: **100% of the cell also satisfies `open.wallets ≥ 10`.** It is the
  many-hands filter with a clock bolted on — and that filter has already died five
  separate ways, two sections above.

**And the ceiling settles it, as it settles everything else here.** Perfect
foresight that the creator will not sell for the next 57 seconds pays **−0.32%
net** — up from −0.89%, and still negative. **The sharpest fact on the sell side,
known perfectly and for free, still loses money.**

**One caveat on that, stated because the margin is now thin enough to matter:** the
±1.05 interval puts its upper bound at **+2.63% gross, which is above the bar.** So
the honest phrasing is **probably below break-even, not provably.**

**And the sell-side signal itself has been downgraded on the real holdout.** In
sample it is **+3.47pp** over a matched clock at the 100th percentile of its null.
On 08-16, fitted elsewhere, it is **+2.04pp at the 83rd percentile, bootstrap CI
[−0.37, +4.20].** The sign holds, the latency-robustness holds, the direction holds
in every session — **the significance does not.** The honest headline is **real in
direction, unresolved in size.**

**One figure is struck outright.** An earlier revision of this paragraph claimed
**"+4.39pp out of sample on 08-16"**. It could not be reproduced under any of four
filter combinations and should not be quoted.

One structural observation from the same work, which bears on whether this market
is beatable by anyone: **wallets appearing in 100+ launches arrive at a median 3.3
seconds, trade at half everyone else's size, and break even** (median 0.0%). So
does the 50–99 cohort, also at 0.0%; every cohort below that loses, by 2.9% to
10.6%. **Nobody in this market makes money by trading it — the fastest wallets
merely avoid losing.**

## What I would do

**Stop building execution.** There is nothing to execute, and the missing pieces
serve a strategy that loses money.

**Do not chase the leads for profit — but do not write them off as noise either.**
Neither reaches break-even and both are in the section above. What survived the
re-run is worth knowing: **flow and sell timing genuinely carry information about
when to leave** — about a point better than a clock at short holds, three and a half
at long ones — and that is the only input in this sprint that beat a
matched-exposure baseline and kept beating it out of sample. It is not enough to
trade and it is the one place a future capture might find more.

Anything that looks like a *new* lead should be put against a matched-exposure
baseline and a best-of-grid null before it is written down. Those two checks killed
every candidate this sprint produced. **And check what your hygiene filter drops
before you trust either of them** — this document's own filter inverted a
best-of-grid null from the 0th percentile to the 100th.

**The recorder is mostly fixed; finish it before recording again.** It used to
write `follow: 60` on shutdown regardless of actual watch time, record no uptime,
cap `highs`/`lows` at 60 and silently freeze the running extreme, and drop failed
transactions uncounted. Those are closed in `tools/capture` (285 tests), together
with sell attribution, full curve state on every candle, and a `capture check`
command that holds every counter to the rows behind it. **What is still missing is
the network fee and cost block**, which the socket does not carry and which needs
offline enrichment against the signatures now being recorded. **And rotate files on launch `t`, not on write time** — the current rule put six
08-20 launches into the 08-21 file, where they sat inside every held-out set this
sprint built, and it leaves only 5 of 7 files starting at a session boundary.
**Keep the socket and add a block sweep that audits it**, publishing `missed[]` as
a list of mints rather than a percentage: the sweep is far cheaper in calls than
signature-paging (142.9 calls a block) but the binding constraint on running one
continuously is bytes — 14.4 MB a block, about 3 TB a day to keep pace live — so it
belongs as an audit rather than as the capture path. **Add two more fields while you are there: the distinct-wallet count
and a SOL-flow flag, carried into the outcome record.** The clean subpopulation above
had to be reconstructed after the fact; those two numbers make the partition free
next time, and they are the only thing this sprint found that separates a real move
from a manufactured one. At 35 launches per
connected minute, **150 coins per cell needs 4 hours per session; four sessions is
16 hours and about 25,300 clean coins.**

~~**One measurement would settle Wall 3 for good** — send 20 real 0.011 SOL buys
and count the fills.~~ **Struck: it has been settled without spending anything.**
Twenty-two months of whole blocks give the failure rate as 11.3% and attempts per
fill as 1.13, which is a census rather than the inference the experiment was meant
to replace. Do not spend the 0.22 SOL.

The machinery is good. The tests are real, the forensics work, the curve maths was
right all along. It is pointed at a market where we are structurally late, and
being late is the thing that costs.

## What earlier revisions got wrong

Recorded because the pattern matters more than the individual errors: **every wrong
number here was load-bearing, confidently stated, and caught only because another
pass went and looked.** Twenty-two entries here, because this list also covers
claims that were wrong without being numbers. **The canonical count of retired
findings was fifteen when this closed and is now thirty** — the row count of
the *Do not quote these numbers* table linked below, which is the register of record
and has gone on growing.

**A companion table lists every retired number and what replaced it:**
[`docs/sprint-2026-08-27/INDEX.md`](sprint-2026-08-27/INDEX.md), under *Do not
quote these numbers*. If you are checking a figure you read somewhere else in this
repository, start there.

- **Wall 3 was wrong.** 1,005,000 lamports was a p90 read as a median, from 25
  samples, and it was a sniper front-end's default preset. The true figure moves the
  minimum viable order from 0.25–0.4 SOL down to 0.026–0.063 SOL. Struck entirely.
- **"Past 60 seconds is untestable" was wrong, in six reports.** `Tracker.adopt()`
  does not re-base the entry price; it reads `coin.outcome.entry`, the 3-second
  price. Verified on 4,083 of 4,083 joins. A 12-hour outcome label was on disk the
  whole time. When it was finally used, it confirmed the negative result rather than
  overturning it.
- **"The tracker polls too slowly" was wrong.** `tracks` and `outcome` measure
  consecutive windows. The real defect is narrower: `adopt()` resets `hi`/`lo` while
  keeping `peakAtSec`, producing self-contradictory rows.
- **"All real structure is worth 0.2 points" overstated it.** It is 0.4–0.9.
- **The one positive out-of-sample number was an artefact.** It assumed stops fill
  at exactly the stop price; filling where they actually triggered turns +1.69%
  into −0.31%.
- **A belief predating this sprint was wrong, and so was the reason first given
  for dismissing it.** The 2.6% → 44% wallet ladder that justified continuing came
  from Dune history for 6 February 2025, not from our data, and on our own data it
  is flat above two wallets. But it was dismissed here as describing "a market
  running 1.7M launches a month against today's 2,851", and **there was no
  collapse**: 43.9 launches a minute in October 2024 against 37.6 in August 2026,
  about 1.6 million a month at both ends. The 2,851 was a count of what our
  listener happened to record, mistaken for the market. The ladder is still not
  ours and still does not replicate; a dead market is not why.
- **The listener drop rate has now been wrong three times and is finally settled
  against the chain.** It was stated as 40%, then as 0–2.5% (inferred from inside
  the capture, which cannot see a launch it never received), then as **90%**. All
  three are wrong. **The recorder drops essentially nothing while it is connected:
  137 of 137 launches on block-by-block comparison, `missing: 0`.**

  The 90% was an off-by-a-denominator, and the shape of the error is worth keeping.
  The events file holds three sessions totalling **10.6 connected minutes** inside a
  span of **100.7 minutes** from first `start` to last `stop`. 353 ÷ 100.7 = 3.5 a
  minute; 353 ÷ 10.6 = **33.2 a minute.** **"Nine launches in ten missing" and the
  89.5% duty-cycle hole were the same fact counted twice.** The baseline was wrong
  too — "~37 a minute" was one hour-average of one day, while the swept windows ran
  79 and 85 a minute and STS's own captured rate peaks at 117.

  The mechanism was simply **nobody running the recorder**, which a `gap` row can
  never show, because gap rows are written by the running process: the interval
  between a `stop` and the next `start` is structurally invisible. "Only two
  seconds of gaps" means the socket dropped for 1.9 seconds during the 10.6 minutes
  it was up. It is not the `mentions` filter and not the dedup path — the
  `mentions` filter is an exact server-side account-key match that never samples,
  so it never *drops* a launch, though it is the reason the *failure* counter is
  inflated 274x (it matches transactions that name the program without reaching
  it — see Wall 3). **The hole is uptime — 3–72% — and nothing else.**

**And these are revision 3's own, found by reading the thirty-five reports side by
side and re-running five of their checks:**

- **The headline fill correction was not reproducible, and it was too kind.**
  −1.67% gross was the most optimistic of every construction of that rule; the
  defensible range is −3.06% to −4.29%. The gap to break-even is 7–8 points, not
  5.7. It also misdescribed the report it was correcting, which filled at
  `min(open, stop)` rather than at the stop level.
- **"76% of peaks within 5 seconds" was one day**, and the quietest one. Pooled it
  is 69.8%.
- **"28–48% of coins fall through −30% inside a second" was conditional** on the
  coin collapsing at all. Unconditionally it is 14.0% inside a single candle and
  20.8% close to close.
- **"Monotone in both directions at once" was not monotone**, and the table it
  described had had the row that breaks it removed.
- **A withdrawn −62% band figure was still being quoted**, inside a subtraction
  that did not add up.
- **The creator-dump line used a 226-creator sample** when measurements of the same
  thing on 3,835 and 5,241 coins were sitting in the same directory.
- **The market-collapse story was invented from our own listener's output** — see
  the 1.6-million-a-month correction above.
- **The 0–2.5% drop rate was measured in a way that could not see what it was
  measuring** — and the 90% that briefly replaced it was a duty-cycle hole counted
  twice. The recorder drops essentially nothing while connected. The problem was
  always that it was rarely connected.
- **The +18.9% ceiling was wrong twice over** — struck a second late, and
  contaminated by the zero-fee actor, whose 16.3% of coins supplied 53% of it. The
  clean figure is **+12.1%**, and the median coin's ceiling is **−1.89%**: the
  typical launch never trades above its 3-second price again.
- **The 93.2% on-chain failure rate was wrong by 274x.** The real rate, counted
  over whole blocks across seven windows and twenty-two months, is **11.3%**. Our
  own listener's failure counter was broken — its successes match a full-block scan
  to within 7% while its failures run 1,426/s against a true 5.2/s. The
  `err`-before-dedup explanation this document offered for it was also wrong:
  replay is measurably absent, 0 of 11,054 signatures delivered twice.
- **The launch-slot stampede was invented to explain a number that was not real**,
  and the compute-unit argument supporting it was inverted — the per-account cap is
  never approached, so it forbids rather than permits the compression it was cited
  for. The mean launch draws 1.44 non-dev slot-0 buys and 62% draw none.
- **The array-cap rule did not only make results look worse — it suppressed a real
  signal and inverted a statistical conclusion.** Re-running the two exit grids
  without it moved the flow grid from the **0th percentile of its own null to the
  100th**, and flow-versus-stopwatch from −0.06 points to **+0.75**. This document
  said flow exits were worth nothing. They are worth about a point at short holds
  and three and a half at long ones; they simply do not reach break-even. A filter
  that deletes the biggest movers does not just shrink an effect — it can reverse
  the sign of the test that decides whether the effect exists.
- **The sprint's own hygiene rule was a selection rule.** `len(outcome.highs) >= 60`
  was issued in the handoff's ground-truth block as a truncation guard, and at least ten reports applied it. There was no truncation: the
  array is a hard cap, nothing was ever frozen, and `peakMult` matches the candles
  to seven parts in a million on all 69 affected coins. It deleted half of every
  genuine 5x in the corpus, made P(5x) look twice as rare as it is, narrowed the
  confidence interval by 29%, and inflated the measured value of exiting early by
  2.15 points.
- **The band correction was itself corrected**, by measuring the one quantity both
  earlier passes had to assume. Every graduation deposits fixed amounts into the
  new pool, so the price drops **20.71% at the instant of migration** — more than
  double the 9% that decided the question — and the median graduate is worth 0.36%
  of its graduation price twelve hours later. Band entry is worse than buying
  everything after all.
- **"Selection lifts both the ceiling and the outcome" was two different rules read
  as one.** They are statistically independent sets moving in opposite directions,
  and the one that lifts the real outcome does it by buying dead coins. Their
  overlap behaves like a ceiling coin, so combining them buys the bad half.
- **Two significance claims did not survive the real holdout, and both were mine
  to publish.** The dead-coin selection reproduces out of sample (−7.11% → −3.77%)
  but sits at the **58th percentile** within exposure strata there, against the
  92.5th in sample — so the improvement is real and its significance is not.
  The sell-side signal goes from +3.47pp at the 100th percentile in sample to
  **+2.04pp at the 83rd**, CI [−0.37, +4.20]. Both are now stated as *real in
  direction, unresolved in size*.
- **"+4.39pp out of sample on 08-16" is struck.** It could not be reproduced under
  any of four filter combinations.
- **The monotone peak ladder is in-sample only.** On 08-16 the peak column tops out
  at the 2–3 bucket. The finish column — every bucket below 1.0, losing 5 to 21
  points — does reproduce, and it is the half worth carrying. That table's headline
  sentence has now been corrected three times, which is itself the argument for
  quoting the durable half and dropping the shape.
- **No twelve-hour result in this sprint has an out-of-sample test.**
  `tracks-2026-08-16.jsonl` — the only genuine holdout — has 82 rows, median
  `watchedSec` 150 seconds, none over an hour. The label is absent there, not thin.
  Every 12-hour number in this document is in-sample by construction.
- **My own verification of the band correction failed, and it failed on units.**
  I searched for a `last == hi` freeze signature, found zero coins, and accepted
  the finding on reasoning instead. The test was broken: **`last` is a price and
  `hi` is a ratio**, so I was comparing incomparable quantities and read an empty
  result as evidence rather than as a broken instrument. The correct signature is
  the curve's hard ceiling — **63 coins sit within ±0.1% of the completion cap and
  zero sit in [0.98, 0.999)** — and on it the finding verifies. An empty result
  from a test you have not unit-checked is not a negative finding.
- **"08-21 is a genuine holdout after all" does not hold — and "there is no valid
  holdout" was wrong too.** The ninety-minute discrepancy between two passes was
  **one coin**: RAMEN is the last row of the 08-20 file and the first coin of the
  session running into 08-21, so one pass measured the gap before it (5,995.4 s)
  and the other the gap after it (611.2 s). Measured on launch time the day-line
  gap is **478.9 s**, and the audit log shows one process running 14.98 hours
  across it. But the corpus does contain a holdout nobody used: **08-16, behind
  3.78 days of silence and five distinct recorder processes.** The one selection
  result that survives every null has already been scored on it — **−7.09% →
  −3.77%** — though 08-16 is a holdout in *time* only: 81.2% of its coins share a
  wallet with the fitting days, and its `funding` block covers 4.3% of the day from
  a single five-minute run, so no wallet-graph finding can be held out there. **No finding becomes more favourable; one negative finding becomes more
  credible.** This was the only correction all night that could have gone the other
  way, and it did not.
- **The recorder rotates files on write time, not launch time.** Median lag 1.00
  minute, p99 17.1. Five coins launched at 23:53 on 08-20 were written into the
  08-21 file and have been inside every "held-out 08-21" set this sprint built.
  Rotate on launch `t`; assign by `t`, never by filename.
- **Three defects in the report that produced the array-cap correction, none of
  them load-bearing.** Its "sevenfold" take-profit ratio does not reproduce under
  any fill window (4.5x or 2.8x instead), though the ~2-point shrinkage it rests on
  is robust; its claim that the volume-consistency test alone yields an identical
  sample is wrong (219 coins fail the low test only); and it says 08-11 and 08-12
  never write `outcome.highs`, when they write 66 and 34 rows — none capped, so the
  no-op conclusion survives. Recorded because a correction is a claim like any
  other.
- **The document told the reader to finish two leads it had already killed.**

Every one of those errors runs the same way: **toward a tidier story.** None of them
changes the sign of anything, and correcting all of them makes the no-go stronger,
not weaker. That is the most reassuring fact in this file, and it is also the
warning: a document that keeps needing to be made *less* flattering was being
written by people who wanted a result.

## The last word

The final lead to be closed was the only positive structural finding in this
document, and closing it says the whole project in three sentences.

There are 86 real winners in the corpus — 0.70% of every launch recorded. They are
not spikes; they are forty-second climbs that half a market walks into, and they
are genuinely there, on real money, with no manufactured price behind them.

> **Perfect foresight on those coins pays +225.6% over sixty seconds.**
> **The information that identifies them arrives around second 20.**
> **You have to commit at second 3.**

That gap — not the fee, not the bankroll, not the latency, not the tooling — is the
entire project. Everything in this document is a different route to it.
